\section{Empty images}

Alencar et al. (2024) \cite{alencar2024zero} address the task of empty image filtering in camera-trap data, formulating it as a binary classification problem to distinguish empty from non-empty images. The authors investigate modern multimodal large language models in zero-shot and few-shot settings, evaluating CLIP, BLIP, and Gemini with prompt-based inference and comparing them against supervised CNN baselines, namely ResNet50-Siamese and ResNet50, on the Snapshot Serengeti, Caltech, and WCS datasets. Their results indicate that few-shot learning substantially improves MLLM performance, particularly for BLIP, which achieved accuracies of 81.32\%, 88.91\%, and 86.09\% on Caltech, Serengeti, and WCS, respectively, approaching the ResNet50-Siamese baseline (81.61\%, 92.92\%, and 88.61\%) and surpassing standard ResNet50, whereas in the zero-shot setting Gemini obtained the best MLLM results (63.53\%, 86.73\%, and 77.66\%). The study also reports that zero-shot CLIP and BLIP showed limited recall for non-empty images, indicating that, although MLLMs are viable for this task, supervised context-aware architectures remained more reliable in the evaluated scenarios.

Mononen et al. (2025) \cite{mononen2025active} address the task of empty image filtering, more specifically the detection of camera-trap image sequences that contain animals while filtering out empty sequences and those containing humans. The authors propose an active ensemble classifier based on movement-masked images computed from short multi-shot sequences, so that classification is performed on background-reduced representations rather than on the original images; the framework combines background removal, deep active learning, ensemble learning, transfer learning, and image augmentation, and is implemented as two binary filters, one for human detection and another for empty-sequence detection, whose outputs are merged to identify animal sequences. Evaluated on global camera-trap data from multiple sites and habitats, the method showed good performance in separating animal sequences from non-animal sequences, including in previously unseen backgrounds and for animal species not present in the training data, while requiring only relatively small training sets; after 12 active-learning rounds, the main training sites reached sequence-classification accuracies of 83\% and 86\%, and comparison with MegaDetector indicated similar detection of animal sequences despite the proposed method being trained with only thousands of images rather than millions \cite{mononen2025active}.

Mitterwallner et al. (2024) \cite{mitterwallner2024automated} address primarily the task of empty image filtering, operationalized as automated detection of whether camera-trap images contain animals, humans, or vehicles, rather than species classification or behavior recognition. The authors evaluate the open-source MegaDetector object-detection model in a cross-regional camera-trap setting, comparing automated detections against manual annotations on 352,426 images collected from 159 cameras in three study regions in Germany, and further assessing the model's suitability for object counting and for downstream temporal analyses based on daily and diurnal activity patterns. The results showed high overall detection accuracy, reaching 96.0\% for animals, 93.8\% for persons, and 99.3\% for vehicles, with only slight underestimation in counts per image on average (-0.01 for animals, -0.05 for persons, and -0.01 for vehicles). In addition, daily detections from manual and automated classifications were highly correlated (Pearson’s (r = 0.996), (p < 0.001)), and diurnal activity estimates were nearly equivalent between the two approaches, indicating that the model can support large-scale, long-term camera-trap workflows with limited manual intervention \cite{mitterwallner2024automated}. 

Banerjee et al. (2022) \cite{banerjee2022sieving} address two camera-trap tasks, namely empty image filtering and animal detection, by proposing a two-stage deep-learning pipeline for camera-trap sequences based on Vision Transformer (ViT) for binary empty-versus-animal frame classification and DETR or Faster R-CNN for animal localization, with particular emphasis on domain adaptation and domain generalization across seen ("cis") and unseen ("trans") locations in the Caltech Camera Traps dataset. For empty-frame removal, the authors report that the best ViT model optimized on seen locations achieved 87.28\% total accuracy on "cis" data and 66.28\% on "trans" data, whereas DETR provided the best generalization to unseen locations with 76.57\% total accuracy on "trans" data; Faster R-CNN yielded lower overall performance for this stage because it detected animal frames well but produced many false positives on empty frames. Based on these results, the authors proposed an end-to-end pipeline that first removes empty frames with ViT and then detects animals with DETR, discarding 99.4\% of all empty frames while losing 0.2\% of frames containing animals; in the detection stage, 87.29\% of animals were localized with IoU > 0.5, indicating that the combination of transformer-based classification and detection can reduce manual screening while maintaining high retention of animal-containing frames. 

Yang et al. (2024) \cite{yang2024systematic} address the task of empty image filtering in camera-trap datasets by conducting a systematic study of transfer learning for binary recognition of empty versus non-empty images. Using ResNeXt-101 as the backbone, the authors investigate not only transfer from source datasets such as ImageNet and Snapshot Serengeti to target datasets including Snapshot Serengeti and Lasha Mountain, but also the effect of different update-layer selection strategies and of the sample size and class number used to build the source model. Their results show that the common strategy of updating only the fully connected layer is suboptimal: with 20,000 target-domain training samples, the proposed optimal update-layer strategy improved accuracy from 92.9\% to 95.5\% when transferring from ImageNet to Snapshot Serengeti, with a similar gain for transfer to Lasha Mountain. In addition, when the source model was built from a binary-class source dataset, increasing the source-domain sample size from 100,000 to 1 million images improved transfer accuracy from 90.4\% to 93.5\%, whereas the number of classes in the source dataset did not significantly affect performance; overall, the study shows that transfer-learning performance for empty-image recognition depends strongly on update-layer selection and on the scale of the source data used to pretrain the model

\section{Species recognition}

Zualkernan et al. (2022) \cite{zualkernan2022iot} address primarily the task of species classification in camera-trap images, within a broader edge-based IoT architecture designed to support near-real-time wildlife monitoring in remote areas. The authors propose an end-to-end system in which images captured by camera traps are classified locally on embedded devices and the resulting animal labels are transmitted through LoRaWAN to a mobile application; experimentally, they compare six convolutional neural networks—InceptionV3, MobileNetV2, ResNet18, EfficientNetB1, DenseNet121, and Xception—trained on 66,400 camera-trap images, and then optimize the best-performing model for deployment on Raspberry Pi, Google Coral, and Nvidia Jetson Nano using TensorFlow Lite and TensorRT. Among the evaluated models, Xception achieved the best performance with 96.1\% accuracy and macro F1-score of 0.87, which increased to 0.97 after oversampling to mitigate class imbalance; however, converting the model for edge execution reduced the average macro F1-score to 0.7 and affected minority classes most strongly. In the deployment analysis, Jetson Nano running the TensorRT version provided the best latency, at 0.276 s/image, whereas Raspberry Pi consumed the least current but required 2.83 s/image, leading the authors to conclude that the proposed architecture is feasible for real-time field use, although data imbalance remains a limiting factor.

Vélez et al. \cite{velez2023evaluation} review deep-learning platforms for processing camera-trap data and therefore cover primarily the tasks of empty image filtering and species classification, while also discussing their broader use in workflows that may support additional annotations such as behavior. Methodologically, the study is not a new predictive model, but a comparative evaluation of AI-enabled platforms—Conservation AI, MegaDetector, MLWIC2 and Wildlife Insights, together with Camelot and Timelapse as auxiliary tools—examining their software requirements, AI functionalities, data-management resources, and output formats, and assessing performance on four out-of-sample datasets from Africa, Asia, North America, and South America. The main findings indicate that species-classification performance was generally limited by low to moderate recall for Conservation AI, MLWIC2 and Wildlife Insights, although precision was high for some species and higher taxonomic groups, whereas MegaDetector and MLWIC2 achieved high precision and recall for binary discrimination between “blank” and “animal” images; based on these results, the authors argue that these systems are most effective in semi-automated workflows, in which AI is used to pre-filter images and human verification remains necessary for reliable final labeling.

% empty image filtering and species classification
Chen and Reibman (2024) \cite{chen2024minimizing} address two camera-trap tasks, namely empty image filtering and species classification, by proposing an end-to-end processing pipeline designed for unlabeled, in-the-wild video data with limited human annotation. Their method combines MegaDetector v5 for initial animal detection, a heatmap-based false-detection rejection stage that exploits temporal consistency to identify repetitive background artifacts, and a few-shot learning species classifier based on PMF, trained on public data and adapted with a small support set from the target domain; in addition, they introduce adjustable K-nearest-neighbor decision rules to trade off classification precision against the number of accepted detections. On Senegal camera-trap data, the heatmap filtering stage substantially reduced false detections, for example from 35.3\% to 8.2\% on shipment 2, from 57.4\% to 12.5\% on shipment 5 ground cameras, and from 72.6\% to 22.9\% when arboreal cameras were included. In the end-to-end classification experiments, combining heatmap filtering with stricter KNN rules improved precision on in-distribution data, increasing sampled precision from 0.556 without filtering to 0.656 with heatmap + 3/5 KNN and up to 0.878 with heatmap + 5/5 KNN, although this occurred with a reduction in projected correct detections; for out-of-distribution data, heatmap filtering remained beneficial, whereas KNN-based rejection did not consistently improve results, indicating that the proposed pipeline is effective mainly as a labor-reduction strategy for difficult, weakly annotated field data rather than as a fully robust generalization framework.

Misidentifications were most frequent among phenotypically similar species, and counting estimates for social herbivores exhibited high variance across observers. The study highlights that observer experience significantly influences accuracy and processing speed, and emphasizes that human interpretation can introduce bias in camera trap datasets, which has implications for biodiversity assessments and population monitoring workflows \citep{zett2022inter}.

The proposed model achieves an accuracy of 93.10\% on the Camdeboo dataset, outperforming several baseline convolutional architectures including ResNet50, VGG19, MobileNetV3, and ConvNeXt. However, improvements vary across species, and performance decreases for certain classes, indicating that the benefits of temporal information may depend on species-specific activity patterns and dataset characteristics \citep{liu2024improved}.

Santangeli et al. address the challenge of efficiently processing large volumes of camera trap images by developing a semi-automated system for detecting individually tagged animals using deep learning. While most prior approaches in camera trap analysis focus on species classification, this study targets the identification of animals marked with patagial wing tags to facilitate resighting and demographic studies. % The literature reviewed in this chapter shows that camera trap analysis has evolved from manual interpretation toward increasingly sophisticated computer vision pipelines. Current research spans supervised image classification, object detection, hierarchical and two-stage recognition frameworks, metadata-enhanced multimodal models, zero-shot vision--language approaches, and operational systems that integrate automated inference with connected sensing infrastructures. Across these directions, transfer learning, object localization, attention mechanisms, expert specialization, and contextual fusion emerge as recurring strategies for improving recognition performance under ecologically realistic conditions.

% Despite these advances, several limitations remain consistent across the literature. Many methods depend on large volumes of labeled data, yet available datasets are often taxonomically narrow, geographically constrained, class-imbalanced, and affected by domain shift across sites, seasons, lighting conditions, and camera configurations. Performance also tends to degrade for visually similar species, small or occluded animals, rare classes, and low-quality images. Multimodal and zero-shot approaches partly address annotation scarcity, but they introduce higher computational cost and do not fully resolve variability across ecological contexts. In addition, most studies emphasize species-level classification, with more limited attention to fine-grained recognition, uncertainty handling, and robust generalization under field deployment conditions.

% These gaps directly motivate the research developed in this thesis.
