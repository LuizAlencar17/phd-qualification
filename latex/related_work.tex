\section{Empty images}

Mononen et al. (2025) \cite{mononen2025active} address the task of empty image filtering, more specifically the detection of camera-trap image sequences that contain animals while filtering out empty sequences and those containing humans. The authors propose an active ensemble classifier based on movement-masked images computed from short multi-shot sequences, so that classification is performed on background-reduced representations rather than on the original images; the framework combines background removal, deep active learning, ensemble learning, transfer learning, and image augmentation, and is implemented as two binary filters, one for human detection and another for empty-sequence detection, whose outputs are merged to identify animal sequences. Evaluated on global camera-trap data from multiple sites and habitats, the method showed good performance in separating animal sequences from non-animal sequences, including in previously unseen backgrounds and for animal species not present in the training data, while requiring only relatively small training sets; after 12 active-learning rounds, the main training sites reached sequence-classification accuracies of 83\% and 86\%, and comparison with MegaDetector indicated similar detection of animal sequences despite the proposed method being trained with only thousands of images rather than millions \cite{mononen2025active}.

Mitterwallner et al. (2024) \cite{mitterwallner2024automated} address primarily the task of empty image filtering, operationalized as automated detection of whether camera-trap images contain animals, humans, or vehicles, rather than species classification or behavior recognition. The authors evaluate the open-source MegaDetector object-detection model in a cross-regional camera-trap setting, comparing automated detections against manual annotations on 352,426 images collected from 159 cameras in three study regions in Germany, and further assessing the model's suitability for object counting and for downstream temporal analyses based on daily and diurnal activity patterns. The results showed high overall detection accuracy, reaching 96.0\% for animals, 93.8\% for persons, and 99.3\% for vehicles, with only slight underestimation in counts per image on average (-0.01 for animals, -0.05 for persons, and -0.01 for vehicles). In addition, daily detections from manual and automated classifications were highly correlated (Pearson’s (r = 0.996), (p < 0.001)), and diurnal activity estimates were nearly equivalent between the two approaches, indicating that the model can support large-scale, long-term camera-trap workflows with limited manual intervention \cite{mitterwallner2024automated}. 

Banerjee et al. (2022) \cite{banerjee2022sieving} address two camera-trap tasks, namely empty image filtering and animal detection, by proposing a two-stage deep-learning pipeline for camera-trap sequences based on Vision Transformer (ViT) for binary empty-versus-animal frame classification and DETR or Faster R-CNN for animal localization, with particular emphasis on domain adaptation and domain generalization across seen ("cis") and unseen ("trans") locations in the Caltech Camera Traps dataset. For empty-frame removal, the authors report that the best ViT model optimized on seen locations achieved 87.28\% total accuracy on "cis" data and 66.28\% on "trans" data, whereas DETR provided the best generalization to unseen locations with 76.57\% total accuracy on "trans" data; Faster R-CNN yielded lower overall performance for this stage because it detected animal frames well but produced many false positives on empty frames. Based on these results, the authors proposed an end-to-end pipeline that first removes empty frames with ViT and then detects animals with DETR, discarding 99.4\% of all empty frames while losing 0.2\% of frames containing animals; in the detection stage, 87.29\% of animals were localized with IoU > 0.5, indicating that the combination of transformer-based classification and detection can reduce manual screening while maintaining high retention of animal-containing frames. 

Yang et al. (2024) \cite{yang2024systematic} address the task of empty image filtering in camera-trap datasets by conducting a systematic study of transfer learning for binary recognition of empty versus non-empty images. Using ResNeXt-101 as the backbone, the authors investigate not only transfer from source datasets such as ImageNet and Snapshot Serengeti to target datasets including Snapshot Serengeti and Lasha Mountain, but also the effect of different update-layer selection strategies and of the sample size and class number used to build the source model. Their results show that the common strategy of updating only the fully connected layer is suboptimal: with 20,000 target-domain training samples, the proposed optimal update-layer strategy improved accuracy from 92.9\% to 95.5\% when transferring from ImageNet to Snapshot Serengeti, with a similar gain for transfer to Lasha Mountain. In addition, when the source model was built from a binary-class source dataset, increasing the source-domain sample size from 100,000 to 1 million images improved transfer accuracy from 90.4\% to 93.5\%, whereas the number of classes in the source dataset did not significantly affect performance; overall, the study shows that transfer-learning performance for empty-image recognition depends strongly on update-layer selection and on the scale of the source data used to pretrain the model

Tan et al. (2022) A related research direction emphasizes object detection as a basis for wildlife recognition, particularly in complex backgrounds or when multiple animals may appear in a scene. \citep{tan2022animal} investigate the application of deep learning–based object detection for automated wildlife recognition in camera trap data, addressing the challenge of efficiently processing large volumes of images collected in ecological monitoring projects. The authors construct the Northeast Tiger and Leopard National Park (NTLNP) dataset, consisting of 25,657 annotated images from infrared camera traps collected between 2014 and 2020, covering 17 species including both wild and domestic animals. Using this dataset, the study compares the performance of three mainstream object detection architectures representing different design paradigms: the anchor-based one-stage YOLOv5 models, the anchor-free one-stage FCOS models with ResNet backbones, and the anchor-based two-stage Cascade R-CNN with HRNet32. Experiments evaluate detection accuracy under different training strategies, including separate and joint training of daytime and nighttime images. Results show that all models achieve high detection performance, with mean average precision above 97.9\% at IoU 0.5, while YOLOv5m achieves the highest overall accuracy. The authors also demonstrate the application of the trained models to camera trap video classification. However, the study notes limitations related to image quality, background similarity, and species with small or occluded appearances, which can reduce detection accuracy in practical monitoring scenarios \citep{tan2022animal}.

\section{Species recognition}

Zualkernan et al. (2022) \cite{zualkernan2022iot} address primarily the task of species classification in camera-trap images, within a broader edge-based IoT architecture designed to support near-real-time wildlife monitoring in remote areas. The authors propose an end-to-end system in which images captured by camera traps are classified locally on embedded devices and the resulting animal labels are transmitted through LoRaWAN to a mobile application; experimentally, they compare six convolutional neural networks—InceptionV3, MobileNetV2, ResNet18, EfficientNetB1, DenseNet121, and Xception—trained on 66,400 camera-trap images, and then optimize the best-performing model for deployment on Raspberry Pi, Google Coral, and Nvidia Jetson Nano using TensorFlow Lite and TensorRT. Among the evaluated models, Xception achieved the best performance with 96.1\% accuracy and macro F1-score of 0.87, which increased to 0.97 after oversampling to mitigate class imbalance; however, converting the model for edge execution reduced the average macro F1-score to 0.7 and affected minority classes most strongly. In the deployment analysis, Jetson Nano running the TensorRT version provided the best latency, at 0.276 s/image, whereas Raspberry Pi consumed the least current but required 2.83 s/image, leading the authors to conclude that the proposed architecture is feasible for real-time field use, although data imbalance remains a limiting factor.

Binta et al. (2023) \citep{binta2023animal} propose a deep learning framework for automated species recognition in camera trap images, addressing the challenge of manually processing large volumes of wildlife monitoring data. The study focuses on classifying herpetofauna—specifically snakes, lizards, and frogs/toads—from camera trap images collected across multiple locations in Texas, USA. The authors evaluate three convolutional neural network architectures: a custom CNN trained from scratch and two transfer learning models based on VGG16 and ResNet50. The dataset contains several thousand annotated images captured under varying environmental conditions, including day and night scenes, occlusions, and small object sizes. To address class imbalance and limited data, the authors apply oversampling and image augmentation during preprocessing. The results show that transfer learning improves classification performance, with VGG16 achieving approximately 87\% accuracy and ResNet50 86\%, both outperforming the custom CNN model. The study shows the potential of pretrained convolutional networks for species recognition in camera trap images, although the approach is limited by the relatively small and imbalanced dataset and by its focus on broad taxonomic groups rather than fine-grained species classification \citep{binta2023animal}.

Schneider et al. (2024) \citep{schneider2024recognition} propose a deep learning framework for automated recognition of wildlife species in camera trap images, addressing the limitation of existing systems that typically focus only on mammals or treat birds as a single category. Their approach integrates an object detection stage using MegaDetector to localize animals, followed by image classification models based on several modern neural architectures, including ResNet, EfficientNetV2, Vision Transformer, Swin Transformer, and ConvNeXt. The models are trained to recognize 25 mammal and 63 bird species commonly found in Central European forests and additionally predict hierarchical taxonomic labels such as genus, family, and order to exploit partially annotated datasets. The training data were aggregated from multiple public datasets and web sources, while evaluation was conducted on camera trap images collected in the Marburg Open Forest (Germany) and Białowieża National Park (Poland). The best-performing ConvNeXt model achieved a mean average precision of 97.91\% on validation data and 90.39\% and 82.77\% on the two test sets. The study demonstrates the feasibility of joint mammal and bird recognition in camera trap images, although performance decreases under domain shifts such as lower image quality or different camera placements \citep{schneider2024recognition}.

Jeon et al. (2026) \citep{jeon2026lessons} address the task of species classification in camera-trap data, focusing specifically on the underexplored problem of maintaining recognition performance over time at a fixed deployment site rather than only across sites. The authors propose a unified evaluation framework, STREAMTRAP, a large-scale benchmark built from 546 camera traps across 17 datasets, together with a streaming protocol in which models are evaluated on future temporal intervals and incrementally updated with past labeled data. Using BioCLIP 2 as the base biological foundation model, they compare zero-shot inference, naive accumulated fine-tuning, oracle-style adaptation, and improved adaptation strategies that combine parameter-efficient fine-tuning, class-imbalance-aware losses, and post-processing methods such as logit calibration, weight interpolation, and interval model selection. Their results show substantial heterogeneity in zero-shot performance, with BioCLIP 2 exceeding 90\% accuracy on 161 camera traps but falling in the 50–80\% range on 162 traps, indicating that site-specific adaptation is often necessary; however, under realistic temporal evaluation, naive accumulated fine-tuning can underperform the zero-shot baseline, averaging 67.9\% versus 81.9\% on a representative 20-camera subset. The study attributes this difficulty mainly to severe class imbalance and temporal shifts in both species distributions and backgrounds, and shows that a stronger adaptation recipe can substantially improve performance, increasing the average accuracy to 84.8\% for the accumulated model and 88.8\% for the oracle setting, while still leaving a gap to the upper bound and highlighting open questions about when and how adaptation should be performed in practice.

Thangaraj et al. (2023) \citep{thangaraj2023automated} address the task of species classification in camera-trap images by evaluating transfer-learning-based deep convolutional models for automatic recognition of wild animal species. Their methodology compares four pre-trained architectures—DenseNet169, InceptionResNetV2, ResNetV2-50, and Xception—whose top layers are fine-tuned on a balanced subset of Open Dataset V6+ containing 6000 images from six classes (deer, elephant, lion, monkey, tiger, and panda), with performance assessed using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. The experimental results show that InceptionResNetV2 achieved the best overall performance, with 94.82\% accuracy, 0.94 precision, 0.94 recall, and 0.94 F1-score, outperforming DenseNet169 (88.38\%), ResNetV2-50 (87.99\%), and Xception (89.26\%). The study therefore indicates that fine-tuned deep CNNs, particularly InceptionResNetV2, can provide effective automated species recognition for camera-trap images while reducing the manual effort required for wildlife image annotation.


% empty image filtering and species classification
Vélez et al. \cite{velez2023evaluation} review deep-learning platforms for processing camera-trap data and therefore cover primarily the tasks of empty image filtering and species classification, while also discussing their broader use in workflows that may support additional annotations such as behavior. Methodologically, the study is not a new predictive model, but a comparative evaluation of AI-enabled platforms—Conservation AI, MegaDetector, MLWIC2 and Wildlife Insights, together with Camelot and Timelapse as auxiliary tools—examining their software requirements, AI functionalities, data-management resources, and output formats, and assessing performance on four out-of-sample datasets from Africa, Asia, North America, and South America. The main findings indicate that species-classification performance was generally limited by low to moderate recall for Conservation AI, MLWIC2 and Wildlife Insights, although precision was high for some species and higher taxonomic groups, whereas MegaDetector and MLWIC2 achieved high precision and recall for binary discrimination between “blank” and “animal” images; based on these results, the authors argue that these systems are most effective in semi-automated workflows, in which AI is used to pre-filter images and human verification remains necessary for reliable final labeling.



% empty image filtering and species classification
Chen and Reibman (2024) \cite{chen2024minimizing} address two camera-trap tasks, namely empty image filtering and species classification, by proposing an end-to-end processing pipeline designed for unlabeled, in-the-wild video data with limited human annotation. Their method combines MegaDetector v5 for initial animal detection, a heatmap-based false-detection rejection stage that exploits temporal consistency to identify repetitive background artifacts, and a few-shot learning species classifier based on PMF, trained on public data and adapted with a small support set from the target domain; in addition, they introduce adjustable K-nearest-neighbor decision rules to trade off classification precision against the number of accepted detections. On Senegal camera-trap data, the heatmap filtering stage substantially reduced false detections, for example from 35.3\% to 8.2\% on shipment 2, from 57.4\% to 12.5\% on shipment 5 ground cameras, and from 72.6\% to 22.9\% when arboreal cameras were included. In the end-to-end classification experiments, combining heatmap filtering with stricter KNN rules improved precision on in-distribution data, increasing sampled precision from 0.556 without filtering to 0.656 with heatmap + 3/5 KNN and up to 0.878 with heatmap + 5/5 KNN, although this occurred with a reduction in projected correct detections; for out-of-distribution data, heatmap filtering remained beneficial, whereas KNN-based rejection did not consistently improve results, indicating that the proposed pipeline is effective mainly as a labor-reduction strategy for difficult, weakly annotated field data rather than as a fully robust generalization framework. 


\section{Behavior recognition}

Lu et al. \cite{lu2023mammalclub} introduce MammalClub, a dedicated annotated dataset of 118,595 images of wild mammals organized into three sub-datasets: a species-recognition set with 12 species and bounding-box annotations, an individual-identification set with 11 species and multi-view images of different individuals, and a behavior-recognition set covering five behaviors—chasing, eating, resting, walking, and watching—annotated through DeepLabCut-based skeletal joint coordinates. To validate the dataset, they evaluate novel recognition models against mainstream baselines: for behavior recognition, they propose a NAS-designed spatial–temporal graph convolutional network using joint, bone, and motion modalities, whose three-stream version achieved 86.98\% accuracy with 0.82 M parameters; for individual identification, they propose the CATLA Transformer, which incorporates cross-attention to fuse local and global appearance cues and achieves 100\% Rank-1 accuracy and near-perfect or perfect mAP for most species. The study therefore contributes mainly a specialized benchmark and accompanying baseline models for mammal species and behavior analysis in wildlife images.

Gabeff et al. (2025) \citep{gabeff2025mammalps} introduce MammAlps, a multimodal and multi-view camera-trap video dataset collected from nine cameras deployed at three sites in the Swiss National Park. The dataset contains over 14 hours of video with audio, 2D reference scene segmentation maps, and 8.5 hours of densely annotated individual tracks, from which the authors define two benchmarks: a first benchmark for multimodal species and hierarchical behavior recognition from 6135 single-animal clips, using video, audio, and scene segmentation inputs, and a second benchmark for multi-view long-term event understanding over 397 ecological events, predicting activities, species, number of individuals, and meteorological conditions. Methodologically, the study combines MegaDetector and ByteTrack for detection and tracking, manual correction in CVAT, dense hierarchical annotation of activities and actions, and transformer-based baselines such as an adapted VideoMAE for multimodal clip-level recognition and a ViT-Base encoder for long-term event modeling. In the first benchmark, joint multimodal prediction with video plus audio achieved the best overall result, with an average mAP of 0.472, improving over video-only input (0.453), whereas scene segmentation maps did not improve the strongest baseline; in the second benchmark, joint prediction over long-term multi-view events reached an average mAP of 0.498, with the best results reported for meteorological condition recognition (0.634) and individual-count prediction (0.593). Overall, the work contributes a field-collected benchmark and baseline results showing that multimodal and hierarchical representations are useful for camera-trap-based species and behavior analysis in ecological monitoring.

Wasmuht et al. (2025) \citep{wasmuht2025sa} address the foundational problem of multi-animal tracking in camera-trap videos, which is intended to support downstream species recognition, individual re-identification, and behavior analysis. Their main contribution is SA-FARI, a large-scale open-source dataset for wild-animal tracking comprising 11,609 camera-trap videos collected over approximately 10 years from 741 locations across 4 continents, spanning 99 species categories, with exhaustive spatio-temporal annotations totaling about 46 hours of densely labeled footage, 16,224 masklet identities, and 942,702 bounding-box and segmentation-mask annotations. Methodologically, the dataset is annotated through a pipeline based on SAM 3 for initial pseudo-annotation followed by human correction and exhaustivity checking, yielding high-quality mask-based tracklets and species labels suitable for benchmarking segmentation-based tracking models. The authors also report benchmark experiments with state-of-the-art vision-language and wildlife-oriented vision models, showing that training on SA-FARI produces improvements of more than 20 HOTA points, indicating that the scale, taxonomic diversity, and annotation quality of the dataset substantially improve the development of generalizable tracking systems for in-the-wild camera-trap analysis.

Humbert et al. (2022) \citep{humbert2022associated} address primarily the task of behavior recognition, in the sense of identifying and quantifying den-associated activities of \textit{Octopus rubescens} from camera-trap imagery, rather than empty-image filtering or species classification. The authors developed a custom motion-activated underwater camera-trap system based on a Raspberry Pi 3B+ and Raspberry Pi HQ camera, coupled with far-red and ultraviolet illumination, and combined it with visible implant elastomer (VIE) tagging to distinguish individual octopuses at den sites. Images were triggered by a foreground-detection algorithm based on a mixture-of-Gaussians background model, and the resulting records were manually coded into predefined behavioral categories and interaction events. Across 785 hours of monitoring, the system produced 249,163 motion-activated photographs and more than 4200 behavioral events, allowing the authors to show that \textit{O. rubescens} was more active during daylight hours, with departures from the field of view (x² = 6.04, p = 0.014) and bottle entry/exit events (x² = 4.61, p = 0.032) occurring more frequently during the day, whereas den fortification occurred predominantly at night (x² = 148, p < 0.0001). The study also documented frequent conspecific interactions without den evictions or obvious aggression, indicating that camera traps can be an effective tool for long-duration behavioral monitoring of mobile marine organisms. 


\section{Multimodal learning for camera trap analysis}

While most camera trap recognition systems rely exclusively on image content, a growing line of work incorporates contextual information into the recognition process. Liu et al. (2024) \citep{liu2024improved} address the limitations of wildlife recognition systems that rely solely on visual features from camera trap images by incorporating contextual temporal information into the classification process. The authors propose a multimodal deep learning framework, termed Temporal-SE-ResNet50, which combines image-based features with temporal metadata derived from camera trap timestamps. The architecture employs an SE-ResNet50 backbone with a squeeze-and-excitation attention mechanism to extract visual features from images, while temporal information such as date and time is encoded using sine–cosine cyclical transformations and processed through a residual multilayer perceptron network. These visual and temporal representations are then fused using a dynamic MLP module to produce the final wildlife classification. Experiments are conducted primarily on the Camdeboo camera trap dataset, containing 9,859 images of 15 animal species, with additional evaluation on subsets of the Snapshot Serengeti and Snapshot Mountain Zebra datasets. The proposed model achieves an accuracy of 93.10\% on the Camdeboo dataset, outperforming several baseline convolutional architectures including ResNet50, VGG19, MobileNetV3, and ConvNeXt. However, improvements vary across species, and performance decreases for certain classes, indicating that the benefits of temporal information may depend on species-specific activity patterns and dataset characteristics \citep{liu2024improved}.

Fabian et al. (2023) \citep{fabian2023multimodal} investigate the problem of animal species recognition in camera trap images without relying on large labeled training datasets. To address the limitations of supervised approaches, which require extensive expert annotations and often struggle to generalize across regions, the authors propose WildMatch, a zero-shot classification framework based on multimodal foundation models. The method uses a vision-language model to generate detailed textual descriptions of animals in camera trap images, which are then matched against an external knowledge base of species descriptions derived from sources such as Wikipedia. To improve caption quality, the authors introduce an instruction-tuning strategy that combines a small set of human-annotated descriptions with automatically generated captions augmented with expert knowledge. The framework also employs hierarchical classification and self-consistency through multiple caption generations to improve prediction reliability. Experiments on a camera trap dataset collected in the Magdalena Medio region of Colombia show that the proposed approach substantially improves zero-shot performance compared with baseline multimodal models and CLIP-based methods. However, the method requires large multimodal models and incurs higher computational cost than conventional supervised classifiers \citep{fabian2023multimodal}.

Alencar et al. (2024) \cite{alencar2024zero} address the task of empty image filtering in camera-trap data, formulating it as a binary classification problem to distinguish empty from non-empty images. The authors investigate modern multimodal large language models in zero-shot and few-shot settings, evaluating CLIP, BLIP, and Gemini with prompt-based inference and comparing them against supervised CNN baselines, namely ResNet50-Siamese and ResNet50, on the Snapshot Serengeti, Caltech, and WCS datasets. Their results indicate that few-shot learning substantially improves MLLM performance, particularly for BLIP, which achieved accuracies of 81.32\%, 88.91\%, and 86.09\% on Caltech, Serengeti, and WCS, respectively, approaching the ResNet50-Siamese baseline (81.61\%, 92.92\%, and 88.61\%) and surpassing standard ResNet50, whereas in the zero-shot setting Gemini obtained the best MLLM results (63.53\%, 86.73\%, and 77.66\%). The study also reports that zero-shot CLIP and BLIP showed limited recall for non-empty images, indicating that, although MLLMs are viable for this task, supervised context-aware architectures remained more reliable in the evaluated scenarios.

Pahuja et al. (2024) \citep{pahuja2024reviving} address the task of species classification in camera-trap data, with emphasis on improving out-of-distribution generalization to unseen locations. The authors propose COSMO, a framework that reformulates species classification as link prediction in a multimodal knowledge graph, where images are connected to heterogeneous contextual entities such as taxonomy, capture time, and camera location; in the implemented model, DistMult is used as the knowledge-graph embedding backbone, ResNet-50 encodes visual entities, MLPs encode numerical attributes such as time and GPS coordinates, and categorical entities are represented through embedding lookups. Evaluated on iWildCam2020-WILDS and Snapshot Mountain Zebra, the method consistently benefited from contextual information: on iWildCam2020-WILDS, taxonomy, location, and time improved test accuracy over the no-context baseline by 3.6\%, 5.7\%, and 2.3\%, respectively, and the location-based variant surpassed CORAL by 1.2\%; on Snapshot Mountain Zebra, taxonomy and time improved over the no-context baseline by 1.0\% and 2.4\%, and their combination reached 96.8\% test accuracy, corresponding to a 3.9\% gain. The study also reports that the framework improves sample efficiency for under-represented species, indicating that structured contextual information can be effectively integrated to support more robust camera-trap species classification.

Chen et al. (2026) \citep{chen2026cameratrap} address camera-trap analysis as a unified multimodal interpretation problem that encompasses species identification, instance counting, and behavior classification, while also extending to structured extraction of ecological attributes and natural-language description. The authors propose CameraTrap-Instruct, a two-stage vision–language training framework built on Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct with LoRA-based parameter-efficient adaptation, in which the first stage performs multimodal projector alignment using a large species–count dataset and the second stage conducts instruction-level language adaptation using an attribute-rich instruction-tuning dataset containing species, counts, date, time, temperature, behavior, and short captions. This design enables the model to generate schema-consistent outputs that include species identities, exact counts, behavior labels, metadata extraction, and concise ecological descriptions from a single input image. The reported results indicate that CameraTrap-Instruct achieves 96\% macro-precision for species identification, 90\% accuracy for exact counting, and 88\% accuracy for behavior classification, while retrieving capture date, time, and ambient temperature with perfect accuracy. The study therefore shows that an instruction-tuned vision–language model can provide detector-comparable performance on core recognition tasks while additionally supporting structured ecological attribute extraction, visual question answering, and higher-level ecological interpretation beyond conventional detector-based pipelines.

\section{Applications of fusion model approaches for camera trap analysis}

Núñez et al. \cite{nunez2019stronger} address the task of wildlife community assessment and population inference rather than empty-image filtering, species classification, or behavior recognition, by comparing and integrating counts obtained from camera traps, visual transects, and dung transects in tropical forests. The authors propose a generalized joint attribute modeling (GJAM) framework to jointly analyze 27,813 counts of 35 species or species groups and explicitly quantify how survey method and species traits affect detection. A central component of the methodology is information fusion at the data level, in which the three survey streams are not analyzed independently or combined by simple averaging, but are integrated within a single generative statistical model that treats all observations jointly on the response scale while accounting for sampling effort, residual covariance among species, and trait associations. This fusion step plays a key role in enabling direct comparison across methods and in producing a unified estimate of ecological responses to covariates such as distance to the nearest village. The results showed that method effectiveness varied strongly by species and trait: camera traps yielded higher counts for nine terrestrial species, visual transects for 11 primarily arboreal species, and dung surveys for six species; trait-based analysis further indicated that visual transects favored diurnal and arboreal species, dung transects favored large species and underdetected diurnal species, and camera traps underdetected arboreal and group-living species. The fusion analysis also altered ecological inference relative to single-method models, for example revealing a significant negative relationship between chimpanzee counts and distance to village only in the integrated model, illustrating that multi-source fusion can provide more complete inference than any individual survey method alone.

Akbar (2025) \citep{akbar2025individual} addresses the task of individual identification in camera-trap data, focusing on feral cats rather than empty-image filtering, species classification, or behavior recognition. The thesis proposes a body-part-based deep learning pipeline in which a ResNet50-based Feature Concatenation Model (FCM) learns discriminative representations from multiple visible regions of a cat—specifically the body, back leg, front leg, and tail—and a YOLOv12 detector is used to automatically localize these parts for downstream identification. The study includes a clear fusion component: the FCM performs early feature-level fusion by concatenating features extracted from the separate body parts into a joint representation for identity prediction. This fusion is central to the methodology because it is intended to make identification more robust when camera-trap images contain only partial views of the animal; however, the thesis also shows that early fusion imposes a strong balancing constraint, since equal sample availability across part classes is required before concatenation, which leads to substantial image exclusion and becomes a key bottleneck of the pipeline. Experiments were conducted on a curated subset of 1,050 images from 10 individuals, selected from a larger collection of 15,881 images and 135 individuals from Victoria, Australia, and the results indicate that the FCM can identify individual cats using all or only a subset of body parts, with the body (flank) providing the most informative cues, while YOLOv12 proved capable of detecting body parts and benefited from further parameter tuning and larger training data. The thesis concludes that, although the proposed pipeline is effective as a proof of concept, the limitations imposed by early fusion in sparse and imbalanced camera-trap datasets motivate future investigation of late fusion strategies for more practical automated deployment.

Wang et al. (2022) \citep{wang2022deep} address the task of animal counting in burst sequences of camera-trap images, with emphasis on improving detection quality as a precursor to more accurate count estimation in the iWildCam 2022 setting. The authors propose two methods built on Microsoft MegaDetector V4: FilterDetector, which refines MegaDetector outputs through an NMS-based post-processing strategy, and DLEDetector, a deep-learning ensemble approach. Fusion is a central component of the methodology. In FilterDetector, fusion occurs at the bounding-box post-processing level, where different filters are combined with an NMS procedure that jointly considers intersection over union and confidence scores to suppress false positives and improve box precision. In DLEDetector, fusion is performed at the detector-output level: two base deep learning models are used to correct and enhance MegaDetector predictions, and their outputs are merged using Weighted Box Fusion (WBF); this fused result is then further cleaned by a binary classifier trained to distinguish animals from background, making fusion the mechanism by which complementary detector predictions are integrated before final counting. On the iWildCam 2022 competition test set, both methods outperformed the best iWildCam 2021 approach and the MegaDetector V4 baseline, with reported improvements of 9.09\% and 6.44\%, respectively, and achieved first and third place in the competition, indicating that ensemble-based refinement of detection outputs can improve animal counting performance in challenging camera-trap sequences.

Amin et al. (2025) \citep{amin2025automated} address primarily the task of species identification in camera-trap images, while also extending the framework to behavior-related semantic interpretation through generated visual descriptions. The proposed methodology combines a Swin Transformer backbone with Faster R-CNN, and replaces standard pyramid aggregation with Bi-FPN to improve detection in low-contrast scenes and for small or partially occluded animals; in this architecture, fusion is performed mainly at the feature level, where the bidirectional feature pyramid network integrates multi-scale representations from the Swin backbone so that local spatial details and broader contextual cues are jointly propagated to the detector head, making fusion central to the improvement of localization and classification performance. In addition, the detection stage is coupled with a multimodal large language model module based on LLaVA v1.5 (13B) for visual semantic extraction, enabling caption generation and zero-shot descriptive support after detection. Experimentally, the best detector, Swin + Bi-FPN + Faster R-CNN, outperformed the other tested configurations on both raw and augmented test sets, achieving precision/recall/mAP of 0.8343/0.8178/0.8161 on the raw test set and 0.8059/0.7919/0.7889 on the augmented test set, compared with 0.6560/0.6694/0.6631 for ResNet50-based Faster R-CNN and substantially lower performance for ViT-based Faster R-CNN. For the semantic extraction stage, LLaVA v1.5 (13B) achieved the highest SBERT cosine similarity (0.6501) and a BERTScore F1 of 0.8978, indicating that the integrated framework can support both accurate species-level detection and useful natural-language interpretation for wildlife monitoring.
