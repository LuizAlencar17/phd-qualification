\chapter{Related Work}\label{cap:related_work}

This chapter reviews related work on the four main tasks of automated camera trap 
processing addressed in this research, identifying limitations in the literature 
that motivate the contributions of this work. We begin by discussing methods for 
filtering empty frames. Next, we examine the automated species identification task, 
considering multiple aspects such as class imbalance, generalization, and strategies 
for improving performance. We then explore approaches to species identification that 
leverage all images in a sequence. In addition, we review existing literature on the 
automated counting of individuals in camera trap images using computer vision. 
Finally, we discuss methods for automated preprocessing on edge devices, independent 
of the specific task being performed.

\section{Filtering empty camera-trap images}

Empty or blank frames represent a significant portion of camera trap data --
for instance, they comprise approximately 76\% of the Snapshot Serengeti
dataset \citep{ss_lila} -- and are often useless. Filtering them out can reduce
human workload \citep{yang2021systematic}, minimize data transmission over
limited-bandwidth networks \citep{elias2017s, zualkernan2020towards}, and save
storage, extending the duration a camera can remain in the field without SD card
retrieval \citep{VELASCOMONTERO2024102815}. This processing step has been
studied as an additional category in classifiers alongside species classes
\citep{villa2017towards, beery2018recognition, schneider2020three,
tabak2019machine}, as well as in a two-stage approach
\citep{norouzzadeh2018automatically, willi2019identifying, tabak2020improving,
norouzzadeh2021deep} where the first stage is responsible for recognizing the
presence of animals and the second for identifying the species. Several tools
have been developed for filtering empty images \citep{wei2020zilong,
janzen2019eventfinder, yousif2019animal, beery2019efficient}, and the challenge
has been explored from multiple perspectives \citep{orn2021computer,
yang2021adaptive, yang2021systematic, yang2021automatic, auer2021minimizing,
alencar2023context, alencar2024zero}.

\citet{norouzzadeh2018automatically} trained nine models to detect images
containing animals in the Snapshot Serengeti dataset, with all models achieving
an accuracy greater than 95.8\%, and VGG achieving the highest accuracy of
96.8\%. \citet{tabak2019machine} developed the R package \textit{Machine
Learning for Wildlife Image  Classification} (MLWIC), which includes an empty
class among the species categories and correctly classified 85.1\% of the empty
images in the Snapshot Serengeti. In its later version, MLWIC2 included a
specific model for filtering empty images, achieving an accuracy of 94.1\% on
Snapshot Serengeti. \citet{vargas2021desert} used an SVM on top of embeddings
generated by a pretrained ResNet50, achieving an accuracy of 99.95\% for the
binary classification of empty frames and desert bighorn sheep.

Zilong~\citep{wei2020zilong} is a tool based on a non-machine learning
algorithm developed to identify empty camera trap images based on color and
edge changes among images in a sequence. It correctly identified 85\% of empty
images and retained 87\% of animal images in the Snapshot Serengeti dataset,
compared to 69\% and 65\%, respectively, achieved by MLWIC. However, the authors
recognize that a major limitation of the method is that it cannot identify empty
images when there is vegetation swinging.
EventFinder~\citep{janzen2019eventfinder} is a similar tool based on background
subtraction for identifying empty camera trap images. The authors report an
accuracy of 96.1\% on their private dataset and a 90\% reduction in human
workload. Animal Scanner~\citep{yousif2019animal} combines background
subtraction with a deep learning image classifier to classify a frame into three
classes: human, animal, or empty. In their study, \citet{yousif2019animal} also
analyze the effects of gradually reducing the model's input size and the number
of filters on the trade-off between model performance and inference time. Other
studies also rely on background subtraction and traditional computer vision
techniques using hand-crafted features to detect animals \citep{zhang2016animal,
lin2014foreground, ren2013ensemble, miguel2016finding, giraldo2019camera,
yousif2017fast}.

A widely used model is MegaDetector~\citep{beery2019efficient}, a generalist
object detector trained to localize animals, people, and vehicles. It helps
users identify animals but does not classify them at the species level, being
effective for filtering empty images \citep{leorna2022human}.
\citet{fennell2022use} demonstrated the effectiveness of integrating
MegaDetector into existing camera-trap workflows, reducing manual processing
time by 8.4×. The early versions of MegaDetector were based on Faster-RCNN,
which was computationally intensive. However, to develop more lightweight
models, the architecture was changed from version 5 onwards to be based on the
YOLO architecture family.

\citet{orn2021computer} compared a DenseNet201 classifier, YOLOv3, and
MegaDetector on camera-trap images obtained from the Cairngorms National Park,
United Kingdom, categorizing them as empty, animal, or human, with MegaDetector
performing best.

\citet{yang2021automatic} used an ensemble based on AlexNet, InceptionV3, and
ResNet18 to automatically identify empty images from cameras in Lhasa
Mountains, China. They trained each architecture on two versions of the dataset:
one using the original distribution and one with a balanced distribution. They
also used thresholding to reduce classification errors. In a complementary
study, \citet{yang2021systematic} studied the impact of the class imbalance
problem on model performance for identifying empty camera trap images using
AlexNet architecture on Snapshot Serengeti dataset. They found that when the
empty image rate was between 10\% and 70\%, there was no significant impact on
the results. However, for rates higher than 70\%, they found that oversampling
helped reduce the error associated with the non-empty images being classified as
empty.

\citet{auer2021minimizing} used an active learning approach to train
location-specific models for daytime and nighttime to detect empty camera trap
images. These models outperform MLWIC by over 10\%. While MegaDetector achieves
5\% higher accuracy, the active learning models use only 150 to 160 annotated
training images, whereas MegaDetector is trained on millions.

\citet{alencar2023context} employed a Siamese network to filter empty camera-trap
images based on a reference image in a scenario where such a reference is
obtained regularly. This approach achieves accuracy levels that are 3\% to 8\%
higher than its counterpart using a non-siamese binary classifier with the same
backbone. In a subsequent work, \citet{alencar2024zero} investigated large-scale
multimodal language models in zero-shot and few-shot learning scenarios,
achieving promising results. Few-shot learning using BLIP~\citep{li2022blip}
outperformed a binary classifier based on ResNet-50, but its performance was
still lower than that of the Siamese version.

Similar to the work of \citet{yousif2019animal}, in Chapter~\ref{cap:filtering},
the problem of filtering empty frames is approached from the perspective of the
trade-off between model performance and inference time. However, while they
analyze this trade-off for desktop computers, our approach focuses on embedding
the models on edge devices and includes model optimization analysis. As in the
work of \citet{orn2021computer}, we also compare the performance of object
detectors and classifiers. As detailed in Section~\ref{sec:rel_emdedded}, some
studies explore empty camera-trap image filtering directly on edge devices.
However, their focus is on validating applications on the edge, whereas our
study aims to assess the trade-off between inference latency and performance.
In Chapter~\ref{cap:bagoftricks}, when studying the long-tail distribution, and
in Chapter~\ref{cap:counting}, within our counting strategy, we consider the
empty class as an additional category on the same classification head, alongside
the animal classes.

\section{Species identification}

Species identification in camera trap images is the most extensively studied
task in the automated analysis of camera-trap data. Early approaches used
classical computer vision techniques, such as hand-crafted features and
shallow machine learning classifiers \citep{swinnen2014novel,
figueroa2014fast, giraldo2017recognition, yu2013automated, wilber2013animal}.
With the advent of deep learning, various aspects of this problem have been
explored in the literature, including the effectiveness of transfer learning for
model training, the impact of training data size, and the extreme class
imbalance typical of biodiversity datasets. Given the static nature of camera
traps, studies have also examined the ability of models to generalize to new
locations beyond those seen during training. Specific approaches have been
investigated to improve classification accuracy, such as localizing and cropping
animals before classification, as well as techniques like confidence
thresholding to improve prediction reliability. Additionally, efforts have been
made to develop models that can run efficiently on devices with limited
computational resources and to create practical applications that allow
ecologists to identify animal species without requiring expertise in model
training or computer programming.

Transfer learning has been explored to train models with limited labeled data.
In one of the first works applying modern deep architectures for animal species
classification in camera-trap images, \citet{villa2017towards} used transfer
learning by freezing the backbone of ImageNet-pretrained models using them as
feature extractor and training only the classifier. They experimented with
AlexNet, VGG, GoogLeNet and ResNet to recognize species in the Snapshot
Serengeti dataset. They also explored fine-tuning, allowing the pretrained
backbone to be further trained with camera-trap data, achieving around a 5\%
improvement over training only the classifier head. In their experiments,
\citet{norouzzadeh2018automatically} found that transfer learning with
ImageNet-pretrained models had only a small positive impact compared to
training from scratch on Snapshot Serengeti. However, they recognized the
benefits of using transfer learning for smaller datasets.

Instead of using ImageNet-pretrained models, \citet{willi2019identifying} 
pretrained their models on Snapshot Serengeti and applied transfer learning to
other camera-trap projects -- Camera CATalogue, Elephant Expedition, and Snapshot 
Wisconsin -- achieving better results than training from scratch. Transfer 
learning across camera-trap datasets is also explored by 
\citet{norouzzadeh2021deep}. In recent years, transfer learning with pretrained 
weights, either from the ImageNet or COCO datasets, has become a common approach 
to reducing training time and improving performance \citep{beery2018recognition, 
schneider2020three, fergus2024towards, pantazis2024deep, pantazis2021focus, 
norman2023can, whytock2021robust, park2024exploring, zualkernan2020towards,
zualkernan2022iot, gadot2024crop, tabak2022cameratrapdetector,
duggan2021approach, miao2021iterative, shahinfar2020many}. We also adopt this
strategy in this work, initializing our models with ImageNet weights.

\citet{schneider2020three} point out that previous researchers trained models
using a massive number of labeled images, such as those available in the
Snapshot Serengeti dataset, but this approach would be impractical for the vast
majority of small-scale projects. Their work evaluates whether deep learning
models trained on modestly sized training datasets can succeed and analyzes
their performance relative to the amount of training data.
\citet{willi2019identifying} also assess the impact of training dataset size on
model performance, finding that transfer learning can benefit smaller datasets.
While using the full Camera CATalogue dataset resulted almost the same accuracy
when training from scratch or with transfer learning, when using only 12.5\% of
the data, transfer learning resulted in an accuracy 10\% higher.
\citet{shahinfar2020many} evaluated camera-trap training sets with 10, 20, 50,
150, 500, 1000 images per class and found that precision and recall metrics
scale logarithmically with the number of training images.
\citet{pantazis2024deep} assess the impact of training set size on
ecological metrics, finding that while smaller training sets can significantly
affect results, some metrics can still be accurately estimated with less data.

Some studies have explored active learning approaches to minimize the number of
images needed to train models -- reducing the manual labeling effort -- while
maintaining high model performance in camera-trap image analysis
\citep{norouzzadeh2021deep, bothmann2023automated, miao2021iterative}.
\citet{pantazis2021focus} studied self-supervised learning to reduce the amount
of labeled data required for training models on camera-trap datasets. In order
to consider the scenario of smaller-scale projects, in
Chapter~\ref{cap:bagoftricks}, we evaluate the methods using multiple datasets
with varying numbers of training images.

The extreme class imbalance resulting from a long-tail distribution is another
challenge in camera-trap data, as it is both prevalent and severe, as shown by
\citet{he2023long}, and explored in the context of species identification.
\citet{villa2017towards} compared model performance using the
original data distribution and a fully balanced dataset, with the latter
achieving accuracies 10\% higher. However, in their experiments,
\citet{villa2017towards} discarded species with fewer than 1,000 images, which
is problematic since rare species may be of particular ecological interest.
\citet{norouzzadeh2018automatically} found that their models obtained extremely
low accuracy on rare classes. To mitigate this issue, they tried three
approaches: weighted loss,  oversampling, and emphasis sampling. They found that
all three methods improved accuracy for some rare classes but not for all,
highlighting the need for further research on the topic. To handle data
imbalance in their experiments, \citet{schneider2020three} added artificially
augmented images to the training set for rare species to ensure at least 100
images per class. They also applied a heuristic sampling method to select
training images, which, combined with the augmented images, can be seen as an
oversampling technique. Despite this approach, they found that species with
fewer than 500 training samples often exhibited poor recall.

\citet{beery2020synthetic} generated synthetic training examples to improve the
models' performance on rare classes, achieving a reduction of 10\% in error
rate for the class deer by adding only 5 synthetic examples, at the cost of a
<0.5\% increase in average class-wise error. \citet{bohner2023semi} included
images from other datasets to increase the number of training images of rare
species; however, the training set was still unbalanced. To handle the long-tail
distribution in their data, \citet{miao2021iterative} used the Open Long-Tailed
Recognition \citep{liu2019large} method to balance learning between head and
tail categories. Other studies have not applied any specific techniques to
address class imbalance \citep{beery2018recognition, tabak2019machine,
willi2019identifying, norman2023can, whytock2021robust, zualkernan2020towards,
zualkernan2022iot, gadot2024crop, duggan2021approach}. In
Chapter~\ref{cap:bagoftricks}, similar to \citet{norouzzadeh2018automatically},
we directly address the long-tail issue using state-of-the-art methods to
improve performance on tail classes. However, we propose a new method that
better balances the trade-off between improving tail class performance and
preserving head class accuracy.

Another challenge arises from the fact that camera traps are static, retaining
the same background for images taken by each camera, which may bias models
toward trained locations. However, a key capability desired in models for the
automated analysis of camera-trap data is the ability to generalize to new
locations beyond those seen during training \citep{schneider2020three}. In fact,
as demonstrated in the literature \citep{beery2018recognition,
schneider2020three, tabak2019machine, norman2023can}, performance is poor when
applied to untrained locations, especially for species identification models.
\citet{beery2018recognition} presented the Caltech Camera Traps dataset,
designed to measure generalization to new environments through a location-based
train/test split, where images from the same location were kept in either the
train or test set. This differs from other works, where the train/test splits
were done randomly \citep{villa2017towards, tabak2019machine} or by capture
event \citep{norouzzadeh2018automatically, willi2019identifying,
miao2021iterative}.

Splitting the dataset randomly or by capture event can lead to data leakage in the
test set, as images taken within short time intervals may be near-duplicates. To 
avoid this, \citet{beery2018recognition} ensured that, when testing on images from 
locations used during training, the training set included images from even days, 
while the test set contained only images from odd days. In recent years, the 
location-based or temporal-based train/test splits have become the standard for 
evaluating classification models on camera-trap data \citep{schneider2020three, 
pantazis2024deep, pantazis2021focus, norman2023can, whytock2021robust, 
bohner2023semi, maile2023successes, velez2023evaluation, beery2020context, 
beery2020synthetic, gadot2024crop, dussert2025being, duggan2021approach}. In this 
work, we adopt location-based splitting to validate our models.

A common issue in camera trap images is that animals appear too small when they
are far from the camera. Some studies have addressed this by cropping bounding
boxes around the animals to highlight them. \citet{villa2017towards} analyze
this issue using a manually checked subset containing only animals in the
foreground, achieving accuracies 10\% to 15\% higher compared to using all
images. \citet{beery2018recognition} compare classifiers trained on full images
and cropped bounding boxes, with the latter achieving accuracies around 10\% to
20\% higher than the full-image model. \citet{norman2023can} also found that
cropping images to bounding boxes greatly improves model performance, with an
F1-score of 0.77 versus 0.47. These results motivated others to train their
models directly on animal crops generated by object detectors rather than full
images, minimizing background influence and reducing bias toward training
locations \citep{norouzzadeh2021deep, pantazis2024deep, pantazis2021focus,
bothmann2023automated, dussert2025being}. \citet{gadot2024crop} showed that
incorporating an object detector into a classification pipeline improves
performance compared to a whole-image classifier, using a dataset of 42 million
camera-trap images as well as smaller datasets such as iWildCam. In this work,
as presented in Chapter~\ref{cap:bagoftricks}, we also conduct a study comparing
the effectiveness of using MegaDetector as a pre-cropping strategy versus using
the full images.

Another investigated approach to improving classification performance is
confidence thresholding, which selects high-confidence predictions while
reducing coverage -- i.e., the number of images for which the model provides
predictions. For example, this can reduce human effort by sending only
low-confidence predictions for manual review.
\citet{norouzzadeh2018automatically} find that using a 43\% confidence
threshold allows them to automatically process 97.2\% of images from the
species identification task at human-level accuracy.
\citet{willi2019identifying} report accuracies between 96.7\% and 98.9\% with a
99\% confidence threshold, while coverage remained between 76\% and 86.5\%.
\citet{whytock2021robust} evaluate several thresholds, finding that for their
models, a threshold of >70\% confidence was required to achieve an accuracy of
90\% or more, with >25\% of data being discarded. In \autoref{cap:filtering}, we
evaluate the model's performance using a precision-recall curve to assess its
accuracy across various confidence thresholds, in line with other works in the
literature \citep{fergus2024towards, velez2023evaluation}. To improve the
robustness of ecological analysis, some studies use a fixed confidence threshold
based on their data to filter out uncertain predictions \citep{pantazis2024deep,
bohner2023semi, lonsinger2024efficacy, maile2023successes, gomez2016animal}.
\citet{dussert2025being} studied the calibration of models through temperature
scaling for species classification in camera trap images, in order to allow the
interpretation of confidence scores as probabilities.

Considering practical use cases, some studies have explored more efficient
models in their research, for example, recognizing that end users may not have
access to powerful computers to run computationally intensive models or that
some applications require models to run on edge devices for in-field inference.
\citet{norouzzadeh2018automatically} evaluate several models, including
ResNet18 -- a smaller version of the well-known ResNet family -- which achieved
only 0.5\% lower accuracy than the best-performing model in their experiments.
This result motivated other authors to adopt ResNet18 in their studies to
balance accuracy and computational costs \citep{willi2019identifying,
tabak2019machine, tabak2020improving, pantazis2021focus}. With the same purpose,
\citet{schneider2020three} included in their experiments architectures
specifically designed for constrained computational resources: NASNetMobile,
MobileNetV2, and Xception.

On the other hand, when high-performance computing resources are available, larger 
models or ensembles can provide improved classification results 
\citep{schneider2020three, norouzzadeh2018automatically, gadot2024crop}. 
\citet{pantazis2024deep} found that the choice of model architecture has minimal 
impact on the ecological metrics used in their study, despite differences in 
machine learning performance metrics. Other works\citep{zualkernan2020towards, 
jia2023neural, zualkernan2022iot} have also explored species identification on 
edge devices by using smaller models and optimizing them for embedded systems, as 
discussed in more detail in \autoref{sec:rel_emdedded}. In this work, along with 
state-of-the-art models, we also include architectures that are computationally 
efficient for both edge devices and low computational power, considering scenarios 
where high computational power is not available.

Efforts have been made to make computer vision models accessible to users with
limited experience in Python or deep learning model training.
\citet{tabak2019machine} developed MLWIC (Machine Learning for Wildlife Image
Classification), an R package designed to be accessible to ecologists, allowing
them to apply trained models to their datasets and train their own models. They
later released an improved version, MLWIC2 \citep{tabak2020improving}. Their
next iteration, CameraTrapDetectoR \citep{tabak2022cameratrapdetector}, was
developed based on the object detector Faster R-CNN, allowing it to not only
classify animal species but also detect bounding boxes directly in R.
\citet{bohner2023semi} developed an AI-assisted semi-automatic workflow in R to
process camera-trap images, making it more accessible to ecologists with less
need for computer vision expertise. Concerned on respecting human privacy,
\citet{fennell2022use} developed a tool to blur human-classified bounding boxes
based on MegaDetector outputs.


\section{Classifying capture events}

Most camera trap projects capture more than one picture per sensor trigger,
hoping to get a good shot to identify the passing animal. Usually, most works in
the literature treat each picture in the sequence as an independent sample
during both training and testing. An alternative to improve species
identification is to consider all images in the capture event to identify the
animal present.

To classify capture events, \citet{norouzzadeh2018automatically} used two
approaches: aggregating predictions from each image within a sequence, and
training a classifier to directly predict capture events. In the aggregation
approach, the vector predictions of images within a sequence were averaged,
resulting in an accuracy score on average 1\% higher than when using individual
images. In the second approach, they trained a model based on ResNet50 using a
9-channel input image, where three images from a capture event were stacked on
top of each other. For this case, only capture events with exactly three images
were considered (around 55,000), achieving an accuracy slightly lower than that
of the individual image model, likely due to the reduced training set size.

\citet{beery2018recognition} used two approaches for sequence classification:
most confident, where the prediction for a capture event is taken from the most
confident prediction among the images in that event; and oracle, where a capture
event is considered correctly classified if any of its images are correctly
classified—an optimistic evaluation. In all experiments, using sequence
information outperformed individual image predictions, reducing the error rate
by between 1\% and 12.35\%. The most confident approach was also adopted by
\citet{willi2019identifying}; however, their work reports results only at the
capture event level, without comparing them to individual image predictions.
\citet{dussert2025being} also explored the most confident and average prediction
approaches. A key distinction in their work is that they evaluated both methods
using softmax scores and logits, applying the softmax function after aggregation
in the latter case. Sequence-based predictions consistently outperformed
single-image predictions, with the average approach achieving the best
results -- improving accuracy by an average of 3.71\% with softmax scores and
3.56\% with logits. 

In their turn, \citet{shashidhara2020sequence} explored training models to
incorporate temporal information from capture events by stacking channels,
similar to \citet{norouzzadeh2018automatically}, but with additional channels
containing sequence information generated by computer vision algorithms such as
MOG2 \citep{zivkovic2004improved} and Optical Flow \citep{farneback2003two}.
Their results indicate that using concatenated channels with temporal
information led to significant improvements -- ranging from 10\% to 20\% -- over
single-image models. However, their work lacks comparison with simpler methods,
such as aggregating scores. \citet{beery2020context} proposed Context R-CNN, an
approach based on a ``memory bank'' to integrate short- and long-term sequence
information for improving animal detection in the current frame. Even though
this method uses temporal information across sequences, it is not directly
applied to species identification at the capture-event level. In
Chapter~\ref{cap:counting}, when predicting at the capture event level, we
evaluated two approaches: majority voting among image predictions in the same
sequence, and average predictions, with the latter achieving better results.

\section{Counting animals}

In recent years, some efforts have been made to address the task of counting
animals in camera trap images. \citet{norouzzadeh2018automatically} treated
animal counting as a classification problem, where each image was assigned to
one of 12 possible bins representing the range of individuals present. The
authors recognized that object detection provides a natural mechanism for
counting but justified their design choice of using classification due to the
lack of bounding box labels in the Snapshot Serengeti dataset, which contains
only count labels at the image level.

In a subsequent work, \citet{norouzzadeh2021deep} used a pre-trained object
detector to generate bounding boxes on camera trap images and counted the
number of animals in an image based on the number of bounding boxes with a
confidence higher than 90\%. With this approach, they were able to achieve a
slight improvement compared to the previous classification approach, but without
using any labeled image from the target dataset. \citet{Kilima2025} used YOLOv4
to count animals in single camera-trap images using a small subset of Snapshot
Serengeti. However, they did not compare their results when using more images for
training.

In 2021, the iWildCam competition proposed a new challenge to encourage
the development of approaches for counting individuals across camera trap image
sequences, providing a dataset for this problem \citep{beery2021iwildcam}. The
challenge explored the scenarios where count labels are not available, which
is the case for most publicly available camera trap datasets, promoting
alternative methods for counting animals, with labels being collected only for
the test set. All the top solutions used heuristics to count animals based on
the maximum number of bounding boxes detected across any image in the sequence,
including our solution described in Chapter~\ref{cap:counting}, which won
the challenge. This approach is also applied by \citet{simoes2023deepwild}.

In the 2022 edition, the iWildCam challenge re-focused on handling only the task
of counting the number of animals present in sequences of images, as it is
considered a particularly challenging problem. For this new version of the
challenge, \citet{wang2022deep} proposed two methods for counting animals:
FilterDetector and DLEDetector, which achieved first and third place,
respectively, in iWildCam 2022. Both methods focused on improving the detection
of animals in images and, consequently, enhancing the overall counting. However,
these methods still rely on the heuristic of using the maximum number of
animals detected across images in the sequence as the counting prediction.

A natural and reliable mechanism for counting animals could be based on
multi-object tracking, as seen in other domains such as pig counting using
top-down view cameras \citep{chen2020efficient, kim2022embeddedpigcount,
huang2023improved}, fish counting with underwater cameras in fishways
\citep{wu2023dynamic}, and fish counting in sonar videos \citep{kay2022caltech}.
However, the low frame rate of camera trap image sequences -- typically just one
frame per second -- poses challenges for applying state-of-the-art tracking
methods. In this context, \citet{arshad2020my} developed an edge device for
monitoring animals, focusing on deer, including a tracking algorithm for
counting. Nevertheless, their approach uses videos rather than image sequences
and lacks evaluation on standard datasets, making it difficult to assess its
effectiveness.

\section{Edge AI for camera traps}\label{sec:rel_emdedded}

Various studies have investigated the use of intelligent camera traps that embed
deep learning models on board to identify animals directly on the edge
\citep{arshad2020my, elias2017s, dertien2023mitigating, hamadi2024exploring,
VELASCOMONTERO2024102815, zualkernan2020towards, zualkernan2022iot,
curtin2019deep, NAZIR2024102453, tyden2020edge, jia2023neural, whytock2023real}.
This approach can provide significant advantages. For example, identifying
species on the edge can be useful for issuing real-time alerts to mitigate
human-wildlife conflicts \citep{dertien2023mitigating} or enable rapid
responses to events, such as poaching or feral cat control programs
\citep{smith2024man, VELASCOMONTERO2024102815}, especially for those cameras
connected to networks with limited bandwidth. Similarly, embedded models can
perform prior image filtering to prevent the transmission of unnecessary
images, thereby saving network bandwidth and energy \citep{elias2017s,
zualkernan2020towards}. Filtering can also help save data storage, extending the
duration a camera can remain in the field collecting images without requiring SD
card retrieval \citep{VELASCOMONTERO2024102815}.

\citet{elias2017s} developed an IoT system composed of camera traps connected
to a private network for performing classification and filtering at the edge in
order to reduce network transfer over slow networks by around 70\%. However, the
model does not run on the camera, but on a compute node located near the
deployment sites in the private network to which the cameras are connected.
\citet{dertien2023mitigating} report the deployment of TrailGuard AI --
an AI-enabled onboard camera trap -- to monitor tigers and review use cases of
this technology for enabling real-time alerts to mitigate human-wildlife
conflict, filtering the species of interest on-board. \citet{curtin2019deep}
developed a prototype of a low-cost \$50.00 camera for wildlife monitoring
based on the Raspberry Pi 3B+, featuring a custom on-board convolutional neural
network to identify snow leopards, humans, or empty images.

\citet{hamadi2024exploring} use the Forward-Forward (FF)
\citep{hinton2024Forwardd} algorithm to train camera-specific models directly on
the camera itself to distinguish between empty and nonempty images.  While FF
requires less memory during training compared to backpropagation, its training
time was more than twice as long, and the performance was 4\% lower.
\citet{VELASCOMONTERO2024102815} developed a smart camera trap using a
Raspberry Pi 4B to filter empty images and identify feral cats. They
trained a model based on SqueezeNet \citep{SqueezeNet} and used OpenCV and
TensorFlow Lite for inference. To improve the model performance on-site, they
retrained the model directly on-board using background images captured at the
deployment site and synthetic training data generated from those backgrounds.
They report that their approach achieved an F1-score of 10\% higher than
MegaDetector. 

In a scenario where only predictions and timestamps are
transmitted over the network, \citet{zualkernan2020towards} evaluate several
computer vision architectures for classifying animal species from camera trap
images directly on a Raspberry Pi 4B connected to a commercial camera trap via a
Wi-fi enabled SD card. They also assess the prediction latency of InceptionV3,
their best-performing model, after converting it to TensorFlow Lite. In a
subsequent work, \citet{zualkernan2022iot} expanded their analysis by including
more architectures and, in addition to Raspberry Pi 4B, embedded the models on
Google Coral and Nvidia Jetson boards, assessing prediction latency, current
consumption, and CPU utilization.

Also using a Wi-Fi-enabled SD card, \citet{whytock2023real} connect a commercial 
camera trap to a Raspberry Pi that uses an embedded model to classify animals and 
transmit alerts over the Iridium satellite network. \citet{NAZIR2024102453} 
investigated the classification of animal species on a low-power customized camera 
trap based on the Raspberry Pi Zero. They used MobileNetV2, EfficientNetV2B0, and 
MobileViT, along with explainable AI techniques, to identify relevant areas for 
classification. \citet{tyden2020edge} evaluate object detection models based on SSD
\citep{liu2016ssd} on a Raspberry Pi to recognize animals, focusing on the
trade-off between inference speed and accuracy. However, the image dataset used
consists of 4,000 instances spanning only 8 species, which limits the scope of
the conclusions. \citet{jia2023neural} used Neural Architecture Search to design
a classification model for identifying animal species in camera trap images on
edge devices, achieving accuracies of 95.35\% and 81.86\% on the NACTI-b and
D3-a datasets, respectively, when tested on the NVIDIA Jetson TX2 board.

In Chapter~\ref{cap:filtering}, we consider the scenario where the camera uses
an embedded model to filter certain images -- specifically, empty frames --
before transmitting them over a limited network or helping to reduce storage
usage. While most of the works focus on validating an application on an edge
device, our main focus is the trade-off between inference latency and
performance, as explored in the work of \citet{tyden2020edge}. However, we
validate our models on datasets that are 100 times larger. Also, we present a
comparative study between classifiers and detectors of varying complexities and
apply model optimization methods.


\section{Final remarks}

Throughout the literature review on automated camera-trap image processing, it was 
possible to identify that the task of counting individuals across sequences of 
images is still largely unexplored. Existing solutions are mostly based on 
heuristics (e.g. \citet{wang2022deep}) and classification (e.g. 
\citet{norouzzadeh2018automatically}) approaches and still exhibit relatively low 
performance, lack interpretable results, and do not consider problems such as the
re-identification of animals across images. We address this task in 
Chapter~\ref{cap:counting}.

On the other hand, the species identification task is widely explored in the 
literature from various perspectives. However, a major challenge -— not only in 
camera-trap data but also in real-world problems in general -- is the long-tail 
distribution and how to improve performance for categories in the tail. We explored
state-of-the-art solutions applied to camera-trap images and proposed a new approach
in Chapter \ref{cap:bagoftricks}. We also identified that, although species 
identification can benefit from using multiple images in a sequence to improve 
predictions, a more systematic study exploring such approaches is still lacking.

Although the task of filtering empty or blank frames is less complex than the tasks 
mentioned above, and solutions based on object detectors such as 
MegaDetector~\citep{beery2019efficient} have shown satisfactory results, we
identified an opportunity to run this task directly on edge devices. These devices 
have limited computational resources, and running large object detectors may be 
unfeasible. With this in mind, in the next chapter, we investigate approaches to 
improve the performance of empty image filtering directly on edge devices.
