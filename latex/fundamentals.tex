\chapter{Fundamentals}\label{cap:fundamentals} 

This chapter introduces the fundamental concepts that support this work. We begin with an overview of key machine learning topics relevant to our study, including computer vision architectures, multimodal large language models, image preprocessing methods, training strategies, and evaluation metrics. We then examine the main challenges and limitations associated with these approaches, situating them within the broader context of our research. Finally, we summarize the core ideas discussed in this chapter and explain how they connect to the remainder of the thesis.

\section{Camera Traps, Workflow, and Investigated Tasks}
\label{sec:camera traps-workflow}

\subsection{Camera Traps}


Camera traps are fixed sensing devices used to record wildlife in natural environments with minimal human interference \cite{burton2015wildlife}. They are typically activated by motion sensors or configured to capture images at predefined time intervals \cite{swann2011evaluating}. Because they can operate continuously over long periods, camera traps have become an important tool for biodiversity monitoring and ecological studies, supporting analyses of species presence, abundance, activity patterns, and behavior \cite{bruce2025large, tuia2022perspectives}.


A camera trap deployment usually produces large collections of images or short image sequences. These data often contain useful records of animals, but they also include many non-informative images caused by vegetation movement, lighting variation, sensor noise, or other environmental triggers \cite{beery2018recognition}. In addition, animal images may be difficult to interpret because of low illumination, occlusion, camouflage, small object size, motion blur, or animals appearing far from the camera \cite{schneider2020three}. These characteristics make camera trap analysis a challenging computer vision problem \cite{beery2023wild}.

\subsection{Camera trap Analysis Workflow}

A typical camera trap workflow starts with data acquisition in the field, followed by data organization, image screening, annotation, and extraction of ecological information \cite{ahumada2020wildlife}. After images are collected from the devices, they are usually grouped by camera location, date, time, and capture event \cite{beery2019efficient}. In many datasets, multiple consecutive images are associated with the same trigger event (Figure ~\ref{fig:serengeti_sequence_example}), forming short sequences that may provide complementary views of the same animal \cite{banerjee2022sieving}.

  \centering

  \captionof{figure}{Serengeti camera trap sequence example.}
  \label{fig:serengeti_sequence_example}

The first computational step is often the removal of empty images, since many frames do not contain animals. This filtering stage reduces the amount of data that must be manually inspected and can improve the efficiency of subsequent analyses \cite{cunha2021filtering}. After empty images are removed, the remaining images may be processed to identify the animal species, count individuals, detect regions containing animals, or infer attributes such as behavior \cite{norouzzadeh2018automatically}. The final outputs can then support ecological analyses, including species occupancy, diversity estimation, activity pattern analysis, and conservation monitoring \cite{tuia2022perspectives, lonsinger2024efficacy}.

In automated workflows, these processing stages are commonly formulated as computer vision tasks \cite{velez2023evaluation}. Depending on the study goal, the system may operate at the level of individual images, cropped animal regions, or full capture sequences \cite{gadot2024crop}. This distinction is relevant because some ecological information may be visible in a single frame, whereas other information may require temporal context from multiple images \cite{liu2024improved}.

\subsection{Tasks Investigated in This Thesis}

This thesis focuses on three core tasks in camera trap analysis: empty-image filtering, species classification, and behavior identification. These tasks were selected because they represent important stages of the camera trap workflow and because they allow the evaluation of multimodal models under different levels of visual and semantic complexity.

Empty-image filtering is formulated as a binary classification problem. The goal is to determine whether an image contains an animal or corresponds to an empty frame. This task is important because empty images often represent a large portion of camera trap datasets and can substantially increase the cost of manual annotation \cite{yang2021systematic, alencar2023context}. In this thesis, empty-image filtering is used to evaluate whether multimodal models can support an initial screening stage with limited task-specific supervision \cite{alencar2024zero}.

Species classification aims to assign an animal image to one of a predefined set of candidate species. This task is central to biodiversity monitoring because species labels are often required before ecological indicators can be estimated \cite{tabak2019machine}. In camera trap data, species classification is challenging due to class imbalance, domain shift across camera locations (Figure \ref{fig:domain_shift_across_camera}), visually similar species, and variation in image quality \cite{beery2018recognition, schneider2020three, cunha2023bag}. This thesis investigates species classification both as a direct multimodal recognition task and as a zero-shot multimodal fusion problem that combines visual and textual similarity \cite{fabian2023multimodal, radford2021learning}.

\section{Zero-Shot and Few-Shot Learning Paradigms}

The classification of animal species in camera trap images is constrained by the long-tailed distribution of natural populations. In these datasets, a small number of “head” classes (common species) contain many training samples, while a long tail of rare or endangered species—often of greater conservation interest—is represented by very few examples \citep{beery2018recognition}. Formally, this produces a strong class imbalance in which the cardinality of the majority set $D_{major}$ exceeds that of the minority set $D_{minor}$ by several orders of magnitude. Traditional supervised learning methods, which assume independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) data, perform poorly in this setting. Models tend to overfit features of common species and lose sensitivity to minority classes, often misclassifying rare animals as the closest majority class in the feature space.

To address the limitations of standard supervised learning, the paradigms of Few-Shot Learning (FSL) and Zero-Shot Learning (ZSL) have been proposed. FSL aims to train models that generalize to new tasks containing only a few labeled examples, commonly evaluated under 1-shot and 5-shot settings, by leveraging prior knowledge through approaches such as meta-learning \citep{vinyals2016matching}. ZSL extends this idea by enabling recognition of categories that were not present during training \citep{xian2017zero}. Both paradigms rely on transferring knowledge from seen classes $\mathcal{S}$ to unseen classes $\mathcal{U}$ through a shared representation, often referred to as a semantic embedding space.

A common method within the FSL framework is the Prototypical Network \citep{snell2017prototypical}. This approach assumes the existence of an embedding function $f_\phi$, typically implemented as a deep neural network, that maps inputs into an $M$-dimensional space where samples of the same class cluster around a representative point, or prototype. For a class $c$, the prototype $v_c$ is defined as the mean vector of its embedded support samples:

$$v_c = \frac{1}{|S_c|} \sum_{(x_i, y_i) \in S_c} f_\phi(x_i)$$

Classification is performed by assigning a query sample to the nearest prototype using a distance metric, such as squared Euclidean distance. This metric-based formulation is useful for camera trap data because it allows the model to represent a rare species using only a few images, without requiring extensive parameter updates.

Multimodal models extend this idea by replacing or complementing image-based prototypes with semantic representations derived from natural language. In vision-language models such as CLIP \citep{radford2021learning}, each candidate class is represented by a text embedding generated from a descriptive prompt (e.g., "a photo of a Panthera onca in a rainforest"). By embedding both images and text in a shared multimodal space, the model performs zero-shot classification by measuring the cosine similarity between the image embedding and the embeddings of the candidate class descriptions.

In biodiversity monitoring, these paradigms provide a way to address the cold-start problem when studying new regions or rare species. When a species has few or no labeled images, multimodal zero-shot approaches allow researchers to define the category using textual descriptions. This approach connects ecological knowledge—where species are described through morphological and behavioral traits—with computer vision systems, enabling automated recognition even when visual training data are limited.

\section{Embeddings and Representation Learning}
\label{sec:embeddings}

The zero-shot and few-shot paradigms discussed above, together with the multimodal architectures reviewed later in this chapter, rely on a common underlying mechanism: the representation of heterogeneous inputs -- words, sentences, images, and, in the methodology developed later in this thesis, entire camera-trap events -- as points in a shared, continuous vector space. This section formalizes the notion of an embedding, reviews how such spaces are learned for text and vision, and introduces the contrastive and retrieval-based principles that the multimodal models discussed in this chapter, and the fusion architecture proposed in Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_03}, build upon.

\subsection{Dense Vector Representations and the Embedding Space}

An embedding is a learned mapping from a discrete or high-dimensional input -- a word, a sentence, an image, or a video -- to a dense vector in a continuous space $\mathbbm{R}^d$, typically of much lower dimensionality than the original input, such that geometric relationships in this space (distance, angle) reflect semantic or perceptual relationships between the corresponding inputs. This contrasts with symbolic representations such as one-hot encoding, in which every category is mutually orthogonal and equidistant, and no notion of similarity can be recovered from the representation itself. The idea that meaning can be encoded in the geometry of a learned vector space was popularized for text by word2vec \citep{mikolov2013efficient} and by co-occurrence-based alternatives such as GloVe \citep{pennington2014glove}, both of which showed that vectors trained to predict word context from large unlabeled corpora capture syntactic and semantic regularities, to the point that simple vector arithmetic can recover analogical relationships between words.

The relationship between two embeddings is most commonly quantified with cosine similarity,

\begin{equation}
\text{sim}(u, v) = \cos(u, v) = \frac{u \cdot v}{\lVert u \rVert_2 \, \lVert v \rVert_2},
\label{eq:cosine_similarity}
\end{equation}

which measures the angle between two vectors independently of their magnitude and is the similarity function used throughout the multimodal models discussed in this chapter, as well as in the retrieval and fusion mechanisms proposed later in this thesis.

\subsection{From Static to Contextual and Sentence-Level Embeddings}

Early embedding models such as word2vec and GloVe assign a single, fixed vector to each word type, regardless of context; consequently, they cannot distinguish between different senses of the same word. The introduction of the Transformer architecture \citep{vaswani2017attention} enabled contextual embeddings, in which the representation of a token is computed as a function of the entire input sequence through self-attention, so that the same word receives different embeddings in different contexts. BERT \citep{devlin2019bert} is the paradigmatic example of this shift, using masked-language-model pretraining to produce deep bidirectional contextual representations that transfer well to a wide range of downstream tasks.

Many applications, including the ones considered in this thesis, require a single fixed-size embedding for an entire sentence or short text passage -- such as a species description or a generated image caption -- rather than one embedding per token. Naively averaging token-level BERT embeddings for this purpose produces representations known to be poorly suited for similarity comparison. Sentence-BERT \citep{reimers2019sentence} addresses this limitation by fine-tuning a Transformer encoder with a siamese or triplet architecture directly optimized so that cosine similarity between sentence embeddings reflects semantic similarity, establishing the modeling pattern later followed by efficient encoder-only architectures such as ModernBERT \citep{warner2024modernbert}, adopted as the text encoder in Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_03}.

\subsection{Contrastive Representation Learning and the InfoNCE Objective}

Contrastive representation learning trains an encoder, or a pair of encoders, so that representations of related (\emph{positive}) pairs are pulled together in embedding space while representations of unrelated (\emph{negative}) pairs are pushed apart, without requiring explicit class labels. The InfoNCE loss, introduced in the context of Contrastive Predictive Coding \citep{oord2018representation} and later popularized for visual representation learning by SimCLR \citep{chen2020simple}, formalizes this idea as an instance-discrimination problem. For a positive pair of embeddings $z_i, z_j$ among $2N$ representations in a training batch, and a temperature hyperparameter $\tau$, the loss is defined as

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{i,j} = -\log \frac{\exp\left(\mathrm{sim}(z_i, z_j)/\tau\right)}{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2N} \mathbbm{1}_{[k \neq i]} \, \exp\left(\mathrm{sim}(z_i, z_k)/\tau\right)},
\label{eq:infonce_general}
\end{equation}

where the indicator function $\mathbbm{1}_{[k \neq i]}$ excludes the anchor from its own denominator and $\tau$ controls how sharply the loss penalizes hard negatives. Minimizing Equation~\ref{eq:infonce_general} over a batch increases the similarity of matched pairs relative to all other in-batch samples, which act as implicit negatives. Multiple variants of this objective exist, differing primarily in how the negative set is constructed and whether the loss is applied symmetrically across both modalities; the image--text objective used by CLIP, discussed later in this chapter, and the per-modality binding losses proposed in Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_03} (Equation~\ref{eq:infonce}) both follow this same general contrastive pattern, adapted to bind a visual anchor to one or more complementary modalities.

\subsection{Similarity-Based Retrieval in Embedding Spaces}

Once heterogeneous inputs are mapped into a shared embedding space, many downstream tasks reduce to a nearest-neighbor search: given a query embedding, retrieve the candidate embedding, or embeddings, closest to it under a chosen similarity function. This retrieval-based view underlies zero-shot classification with CLIP, in which an image embedding is compared against a fixed set of class-description embeddings, and more generally underlies open-vocabulary recognition, in which the candidate set can be extended simply by embedding new textual descriptions, without retraining the encoder. At the scale of large embedding collections, exact nearest-neighbor search becomes computationally expensive, which has motivated approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) methods and libraries such as FAISS \citep{johnson2019billion}, which trade a small amount of retrieval accuracy for substantial gains in query speed and memory efficiency through techniques such as product quantization and graph-based indexing. Framing classification, matching, and retrieval as instances of the same operation -- similarity search over a learned embedding space -- is a recurring pattern across the multimodal models reviewed in this chapter, and forms the basis of the hierarchical retrieval mechanism proposed in Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_03}.

\section{Multimodal Large Language Models}
\subsection{CLIP}

Contrastive Language–Image Pre-training (CLIP), introduced in \citep{radford2021learning}, departs from traditional supervised computer vision by replacing fixed, manually annotated label sets with large-scale natural language supervision. Instead of predicting a category from a predefined list, CLIP learns from approximately 400 million image–text pairs collected from the internet. The key idea is that natural language provides a richer supervisory signal than curated taxonomies such as ImageNet classes. Text associated with images can encode object names, attributes, relationships, actions, and contextual information. By leveraging large-scale image–text data, CLIP learns visual representations aligned with language, which improves transfer across tasks and domains.

Technically, CLIP consists of two neural networks trained jointly: an image encoder (either a modified ResNet or a Vision Transformer) and a Transformer-based text encoder (Figure \ref{fig:clip_architecture}). Each encoder maps its input into a latent vector, and both outputs are projected into a shared multimodal embedding space where cosine similarity measures semantic alignment. Instead of generating captions or predicting tokens sequentially, CLIP uses a contrastive objective. Given a minibatch of $N$ image–text pairs, the model learns to identify the correct pairing among the $N^2$ possible combinations. The training objective increases similarity between matched pairs and decreases similarity between mismatched ones using a symmetric cross-entropy loss over temperature-scaled cosine similarities. This formulation improves training efficiency compared to generative captioning methods and enables stable large-scale training without supervised pretraining.

\begin{figure*}[!ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=6in]{images/clip.png}
\caption{CLIP architecture}
\legend{Source: \citet{radford2021learning}.}
\label{fig:clip_architecture}
\end{figure*}

A key property of CLIP is its ability to perform zero-shot transfer. Because the model learns a joint embedding space aligned with natural language, it can be applied to new classification tasks without additional training. To classify images in a new dataset, textual prompts describing each class (e.g., “a photo of a dog”) are encoded using the text encoder and compared to image embeddings using cosine similarity. This produces a linear classifier whose weights are generated from language. In zero-shot evaluation on ImageNet, CLIP achieves 76.2\% top-1 accuracy, matching the original supervised ResNet-50 despite using none of ImageNet’s labeled training examples \citep{radford2021learning}. Across more than 30 datasets—including object recognition, fine-grained classification, action recognition, optical character recognition, and geolocation—CLIP often performs competitively with supervised baselines and sometimes surpasses linear probes trained on standard ImageNet features.

CLIP also exhibits several empirical properties. Its zero-shot performance scales predictably with model size and compute. Larger Vision Transformer variants are more compute-efficient than convolutional models, and representation learning evaluations show that CLIP features perform well across diverse benchmarks. However, the model struggles with tasks requiring precise spatial reasoning, counting, or specialized domain knowledge, indicating that semantic alignment alone does not guarantee structured reasoning capabilities \citep{radford2021learning}. Overall, CLIP demonstrates that large-scale multimodal contrastive learning can produce open-vocabulary visual systems with strong generalization ability.

\subsection{BLIP}

Bootstrapping Language–Image Pre-training (BLIP) is a vision–language framework designed to improve multimodal models on both understanding and generation tasks. Many vision–language systems focus on a single task, such as image–text retrieval or captioning. BLIP addresses this limitation by introducing a unified architecture that supports multiple multimodal tasks. The framework also proposes a data bootstrapping strategy to improve the quality of large-scale image–text datasets collected from the web, which often contain noisy or inaccurate captions \citep{li2022blip}.

The concepts related to BLIP described in this section are based on \citep{li2022blip}.

BLIP is based on a Multimodal Mixture of Encoder–Decoder (MED) architecture. The model combines a visual encoder, typically a Vision Transformer (ViT), with a text transformer that processes textual inputs and interacts with visual representations through cross-attention layers (Figure \ref{fig:blip_architecture}). This design allows the system to operate in different modes depending on the task. It can function as a unimodal encoder for representation learning, an image-grounded text encoder for multimodal understanding, or an image-grounded text decoder for text generation. As a result, BLIP can learn relationships between visual content and language while also generating text conditioned on images.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=6in]{images/blip.png}
\caption{BLIP architecture}
\legend{Source: \citet{li2022blip}.}
\label{fig:blip_architecture}

During pre-training, BLIP optimizes three complementary objectives that align images and text. The image–text contrastive objective aligns visual and textual representations in a shared embedding space and improves retrieval performance. The image–text matching objective predicts whether a given image and text pair correspond to each other, encouraging the model to capture fine-grained multimodal relationships. Finally, a language modeling objective enables the model to generate textual descriptions from images using an autoregressive decoding process. Together, these objectives allow the model to perform both multimodal understanding and generation tasks within a single framework.

Another contribution of BLIP is its Captioning and Filtering data bootstrapping strategy. Image–text pairs collected from the web often contain captions that do not accurately describe the visual content. To address this problem, BLIP uses a captioning model to generate synthetic captions and a filtering model to remove low-quality text descriptions. This process produces a cleaner training dataset and improves performance on tasks such as image–text retrieval, image captioning, and visual question answering.

\subsection{GPT}

The Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT) is a family of large language models designed to understand and generate natural language. GPT models are based on the Transformer architecture and are trained on large text corpora to learn statistical patterns in language. During training, the model predicts the next token in a sequence given the previous context, enabling it to generate coherent text. Because of this autoregressive formulation, GPT can perform many language tasks without task-specific architectures \citep{achiam2023gpt}.

The following description of the GPT architecture, training process, and capabilities is based on \citep{achiam2023gpt}.

GPT models use a Transformer architecture with self-attention mechanisms to capture relationships between tokens in a sequence. During training, the model processes large volumes of text and learns representations that capture syntactic, semantic, and contextual information. Training typically includes a large-scale pretraining phase using unlabeled text, followed by post-training methods such as reinforcement learning from human feedback, which improves instruction following and aligns outputs with human preferences.

Due to their ability to process and generate language, GPT models can be applied to many natural language processing tasks, including text generation, question answering, summarization, translation, code generation, and conversational systems. Recent versions also support multimodal inputs and interaction with external tools, enabling more complex reasoning and task execution.

Experimental evaluations show that modern GPT models achieve strong performance across many benchmarks (Figure \ref{fig:gpt_results}). For example, GPT-4 outperforms earlier language models on several tasks and reaches human-level performance on some standardized exams and professional assessments. Reported results show competitive performance across domains such as science, mathematics, law, and general knowledge.

\subsection{Gemini}

Gemini is a family of multimodal artificial intelligence models designed to support reasoning, language understanding, and cross-modal processing. Unlike traditional language models that operate primarily on text, Gemini was designed as a multimodal system capable of processing text, images, audio, and video \citep{team2023gemini}. The model family includes three main variants: Gemini Ultra, Gemini Pro, and Gemini Nano. These versions target different computational environments, ranging from high-performance systems to on-device deployment.

The Gemini architecture is based on an enhanced decoder-only Transformer with improvements for stable large-scale training. The models support long context windows of up to 32,000 tokens and use attention optimizations such as multi-query attention to improve efficiency. A key feature is the ability to process interleaved multimodal inputs, meaning that text, images, audio, and video can appear within the same sequence (Figure \ref{fig:gemini_architecture}). Visual and video inputs are represented as sequences of frames, while audio inputs are encoded using speech representations. This design allows the model to integrate information from multiple modalities within a single framework \citep{team2023gemini}.

\centering

\caption{Illustration of the multimodal reasoning capabilities of Gemini models.}
\legend{Source: \citet{team2023gemini}.}
\label{fig:gemini_architecture}

Gemini models are trained on large multimodal and multilingual datasets that include web documents, books, code repositories, images, audio, and video. The training pipeline includes filtering procedures and safety mechanisms to improve data quality. During pretraining, the model learns representations that capture relationships across modalities, enabling tasks such as multimodal reasoning, content generation, summarization, and information extraction \citep{team2023gemini}. After pretraining, additional stages such as instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback improve instruction following and output quality.

Experimental results indicate that Gemini models perform well across benchmarks involving reasoning, mathematics, coding, and multimodal understanding. Gemini Ultra, for example, achieves approximately 90\% accuracy on the MMLU benchmark and reports strong performance on tasks involving text, images, audio, and video. The model also performs well on mathematical benchmarks such as GSM8K and coding benchmarks such as HumanEval \citep{team2023gemini}. These results show the potential of multimodal foundation models for applications in areas including education, software development, accessibility, and intelligent assistants.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
The evaluation of model performance is an essential component of deep learning research. A single scalar metric often does not capture all aspects of model behavior, especially in high-dimensional problems. This section presents the main mathematical and conceptual tools used to evaluate classification models, with emphasis on metrics that remain informative under class imbalance.

\subsection{Classification Metrics}

Classification performance is typically summarized using a confusion matrix, which divides predictions into True Positives ($TP$), True Negatives ($TN$), False Positives ($FP$), and False Negatives ($FN$). These counts provide a basic description of model predictions, but normalized metrics are required for meaningful comparison between models.

\subsubsection{Accuracy}

Accuracy measures the proportion of correctly classified observations:
\begin{equation}
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}
\end{equation}

Although widely used, accuracy can be misleading in datasets with strong class imbalance. For example, if 99\% of samples belong to the majority class, a classifier that always predicts that class achieves 99\% accuracy while failing to identify minority instances.

\subsubsection{Precision, Recall, and the F1-Score}

Additional metrics evaluate performance on the positive class:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Precision (Positive Predictive Value):} Measures the proportion of predicted positive instances that are correct.
    \begin{equation}
    \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}
    \end{equation}
    
    \item \textbf{Recall (Sensitivity):} Measures the proportion of actual positive instances correctly identified by the model.
    \begin{equation}
    \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}
    \end{equation}
    
    \item \textbf{F1-Score:} The harmonic mean of precision and recall. This metric balances both measures and penalizes large differences between them.
    \begin{equation}
    F_1 = 2 \cdot \frac{\text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

The choice among these metrics depends on the cost of different types of errors. For example, medical diagnosis often prioritizes recall to reduce missed cases (false negatives), while spam detection may prioritize precision to avoid misclassifying legitimate messages.

\subsection{Precision–Recall Curve}

Many deep learning models output probabilities rather than discrete labels. A final prediction is obtained by applying a classification threshold $\tau$ (commonly set to 0.5). However, the optimal threshold depends on the application.

\subsubsection{Threshold Trade-off}

The Precision–Recall (PR) curve plots precision against recall for different threshold values $\tau \in [0,1]$. As the threshold decreases, the model predicts more positive cases. This usually increases recall but may reduce precision because more false positives are introduced.

\subsubsection{Area Under the Precision–Recall Curve (AUPRC)}

The AUPRC, also known as Average Precision, summarizes the PR curve with a single value:
\begin{equation}
\text{AUPRC} = \sum_{n} (R_n - R_{n-1}) P_n
\end{equation}
where $P_n$ and $R_n$ represent precision and recall at the $n$-th threshold.

\subsubsection{PR vs.\ ROC in Imbalanced Scenarios}

The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve is commonly used for classification evaluation, but it can be less informative when classes are highly imbalanced. In such cases, the False Positive Rate ($FP/(FP+TN)$) may remain small due to the large number of true negatives. The PR curve does not include $TN$ in its main axes, which makes it more informative for tasks involving rare events or anomaly detection.

This chapter presented the theoretical and technical foundations required for wildlife classification, covering topics from basic deep learning concepts to multimodal systems such as CLIP, BLIP, and Gemini. The transition from unimodal computer vision to vision–language models illustrates how aligning textual and visual representations can improve model generalization. The chapter also emphasized evaluation metrics suited to imbalanced datasets, particularly the Precision–Recall curve and the Area Under the Precision–Recall Curve, which are relevant for datasets where rare species are underrepresented. Together, these concepts provide the theoretical basis and methodological tools for the research problem. The multimodal modeling strategies and training protocols described here are employed choices in the following chapters and support the development of automated species classification systems for biodiversity monitoring.

The next chapter reviews the literature on automated camera trap analysis, with emphasis on empty-image filtering, species recognition, and behavior analysis. It examines the evolution from supervised computer vision methods to multimodal foundation models and fusion-based approaches, identifying the strengths and limitations of existing techniques. This review establishes the research context for the experiments proposed in this thesis and highlights the gaps that motivate the methodologies presented in the subsequent chapters.
