\chapter[Introduction]{Introduction}

Wildlife monitoring provides essential information for ecology, conservation biology, and environmental management. It supports the study of species distribution, population dynamics, ecosystem change, and the effects of human activities and climate change on biodiversity~\citep{ahumada2013monitoring, he2016visual, tuia2022perspectives, bruce2025large, lindenmayer2022we}. Data for these analyses can be collected through different sensing and observation systems, including remote sensing, GPS tags, camera traps, bioacoustic recorders, and citizen-science platforms such as iNaturalist~\citep{horn2018inat, shepley2021automated, herrera2025inaturalist} and eButterfly~\citep{prudic2017ebutterfly}. Among these instruments, camera traps have become particularly important because they allow non-invasive and continuous observation of animals in natural environments.

\section{Context and Motivation}

Camera traps are established, non-invasive, and cost-effective tools for recording wildlife in situ. The images and videos produced by these devices support ecological analyses of terrestrial vertebrate communities, including studies of species occupancy, diversity, abundance, density, community composition, and behavior~\citep{ahumada2013monitoring, beery2021iwildcam, tanwar2021camera, delisle2021next}. Because camera traps are typically activated by motion or programmed to capture images at scheduled intervals, a single deployment can produce large collections of images or short image sequences~\citep{he2016visual}.

The scale of these datasets creates a major processing bottleneck. For example, the Snapshot Serengeti project collected 3.2 million images between June 2010 and May 2013 across six seasons~\citep{swanson2015snapshot}, and this collection has since grown to 7.1 million images over eleven seasons~\citep{ss_lila}. Similarly, the Caltech Camera Traps dataset contains 243,100 images from 140 camera locations in the Southwestern United States, annotated across 21 animal categories plus empty images, with approximately 66,000 bounding-box annotations. Around 70\% of its images are labeled as empty~\citep{beery2018recognition}. The annotation burden grows with this scale. The original Snapshot Serengeti labeling effort required more than 17,000 volunteer hours~\citep{norouzzadeh2018automatically}, and comparable manual effort is often impractical for smaller research groups, long-term monitoring programs, or projects focused on rare and less frequently observed species.

Deep learning has been widely adopted to reduce this manual effort. Existing methods have been applied to empty-image filtering~\citep{mononen2025active, yousif2019animal, yang2024systematic, cunha2021filtering}, species classification~\citep{chen2023applying, velez2023evaluation, beery2018recognition, norouzzadeh2018automatically}, individual counting~\citep{norouzzadeh2018automatically, norouzzadeh2021deep, arshad2020my, wang2022deep}, and behavior recognition~\citep{schindler2021identification, bain2021automated, binta2023animal, lu2023mammalclub}. In addition, platforms such as MegaDetector~\citep{beery2019efficient}, MLWIC2~\citep{tabak2020improving}, Wildlife Insights~\citep{ahumada2020wildlife}, Conservation AI~\citep{chalmers2019conservation}, IFLA~\citep{xi2021image}, and Animal Scanner~\citep{yousif2019animal} have made automated camera trap processing more accessible to researchers and practitioners.

Despite these advances, many supervised computer-vision systems still depend on large annotated datasets from the target domain. This dependence is problematic in biodiversity monitoring because new deployments often involve different species, camera locations, lighting conditions, backgrounds, and sampling protocols. As a result, models trained in one setting may not generalize well to another~\citep{beery2021scaling, yang2021systematic, zhu2022class, malik2021two, cunha2023bag}. These limitations motivate the investigation of approaches that can operate with little or no task-specific training data.

Pre-trained multimodal models, especially vision-language models, offer a promising direction for this problem. By aligning visual and textual representations, these models can use natural-language descriptions, prompts, or external textual knowledge to perform recognition tasks beyond the labels seen during training. This property makes them relevant for zero-shot and few-shot learning settings, where the objective is to reduce dependence on large labeled datasets. In the context of camera trap workflows, such models may support core tasks such as empty-image filtering, species classification, and behavior identification while requiring less task-specific supervision than conventional supervised pipelines.

Therefore, the gap addressed in this thesis is not only the lack of automation for camera trap data, but the limited understanding of how pre-trained multimodal models behave when applied to ecological vision tasks under realistic low-supervision constraints. Existing supervised systems can be effective when sufficient annotated data are available, but their dependence on target-domain labels limits transfer to new deployments. At the same time, vision-language models offer zero-shot and few-shot capabilities, but their reliability across camera trap tasks, datasets, image conditions, and fusion strategies remains insufficiently characterized. This thesis addresses this gap by evaluating multimodal models across core workflow tasks and by investigating whether visual-textual fusion can improve zero-shot species classification.

\section{Challenges in Computer Vision for camera trap Data Analysis}

Extracting reliable information from camera trap images is difficult because the visual conditions are often uncontrolled. Animals may appear far from the camera, partially occluded, camouflaged, blurred by motion, or captured under poor illumination~\citep{villa2017towards, norouzzadeh2018automatically, beery2018recognition, gomez2016animal}. In some cases, only part of the animal is visible, or the image contains cluttered vegetation and background structures that make visual interpretation difficult even for specialists.

camera trap datasets also present statistical and ecological challenges. They often contain many empty images, strong class imbalance, long-tailed species distributions, annotation errors, and temporal or spatial dependence among observations~\citep{norouzzadeh2018automatically, schneider2020three, brookes2025panaf, bevan2025deep}. Rare species may appear in only a small number of images, while common species dominate the training data. This imbalance can reduce sensitivity to species of high conservation interest.

Domain generalization is another central challenge. Because camera traps are stationary, models can learn correlations between species labels and site-specific backgrounds in the training data. These correlations may not hold when the same model is deployed in a new location or season~\citep{beery2018recognition, schneider2020three, tabak2019machine, norman2023can}. Consequently, high performance on a familiar dataset does not necessarily imply robust performance in a new ecological setting.

These challenges directly motivate the low-supervision perspective adopted in this thesis. Zero-shot and few-shot strategies are relevant because they test whether pre-trained models can transfer general visual and semantic knowledge to camera trap tasks with limited adaptation. Multimodal models are particularly relevant because textual descriptions, class names, prompts, and species knowledge can provide semantic information when visual training examples are scarce.

\section{Computer Vision Tasks in Automated camera trap Workflows}

Before camera trap data can support ecological analyses, raw images must be processed to extract information required by the study. This information may include whether an animal is present, which species appears in the image, how many individuals are present, and which behavior is being performed~\citep{norouzzadeh2018automatically}. These processing steps can be formulated as computer-vision tasks and organized as part of a camera trap workflow.

A first practical task is empty-image filtering. Many camera trap frames are empty because motion sensors can be triggered by vegetation movement, lighting changes, or other environmental factors. Filtering these images reduces the amount of data that must be manually inspected and can serve as an initial stage before downstream analyses~\citep{beery2019efficient, gadot2024crop, velez2023evaluation, leorna2022human}. In this thesis, empty-image filtering is used to evaluate whether multimodal models can support an early screening step under zero-shot and few-shot settings.

A second task is species classification. Species recognition is central to biodiversity monitoring because species labels are often required before estimating ecological indicators such as richness, occupancy, and activity patterns. Although species classification is a classical computer-vision problem, camera trap data introduce specific difficulties, including class imbalance~\citep{schneider2020three, johnson2019survey, li2024deep}, few-shot scenarios~\citep{alencar2024zero}, fine-grained visual differences between species~\citep{van2017devil}, and domain shift across camera locations. This thesis investigates species classification both as a direct multimodal recognition task and as a zero-shot multimodal fusion problem.

A third task is behavior identification. Behavior recognition is important because ecological studies often require information about animal activity, such as feeding, resting, or moving. Compared with empty-image filtering and species classification, behavior identification can be more dependent on posture, context, and temporal information. For this reason, this thesis considers both single-image inference and sequence-based inference when evaluating behavior identification.

Together, these tasks represent a simplified but practical camera trap workflow: identifying whether an image contains an animal, recognizing the species, and inferring behavioral information when possible. This structure supports the central objective of the thesis by allowing pre-trained multimodal models to be evaluated across tasks with different levels of visual, semantic, and contextual complexity.

These tasks motivate two complementary research directions in this thesis. The first direction evaluates whether pre-trained multimodal models can operate across multiple stages of the camera trap workflow under zero-shot and few-shot settings, thereby testing their potential as flexible components for low-supervision biodiversity monitoring. The second direction focuses on species classification and investigates whether combining visual similarity with language-based textual evidence can improve zero-shot recognition without fine-tuning on the target datasets. Together, these directions define the central research problem: under which conditions can multimodal models and visual-textual fusion reduce dependence on annotated camera trap data while remaining reliable for practical ecological workflows?

\section{Research Hypotheses}

This thesis is guided by three research hypotheses concerning the use of pre-trained multimodal models in camera trap workflows under low-supervision regimes.

\begin{description}
\item[H1] Pre-trained multimodal models can support core camera trap tasks, including empty-image filtering, species classification, and behavior identification, with limited or no task-specific supervision, although their effectiveness varies across tasks and model architectures.

\item[H2] In zero-shot species classification, combining CLIP-based visual similarity with language-based textual similarity can improve top-1 accuracy relative to standalone visual similarity and competitive zero-shot baselines, but the magnitude and consistency of the gains depend on the dataset, image setting, and species characteristics.

\item[H3] The usefulness of multimodal models in camera trap workflows is conditional rather than universal: performance is expected to depend on supervision regime, prompt design, temporal context, image quality, species-level visual distinctiveness, and the quality of external textual knowledge.
\end{description}


\section{Objectives}

\subsection{General Objective}

To investigate the conditions under which multimodal machine learning models can support and improve the automation of camera trap workflows for biodiversity monitoring, with emphasis on ecological vision tasks under low-supervision and zero-shot settings.

\subsection{Specific Objectives}

\begin{description}

\item[1.] To evaluate the potential of multimodal foundation models for core camera trap tasks, including empty-image filtering, species classification, and behavior identification.

\item[2.] To assess how the performance of multimodal models varies across task types, supervision regimes, and input contexts in camera trap data.

\item[3.] To propose and evaluate a zero-shot multimodal fusion strategy for species classification based on the integration of visual similarity and textual similarity.

\item[4.] To analyze the conditions and limitations that affect the effectiveness of multimodal models and visual-textual fusion in camera trap workflows.

\item[5.] To evaluate practical constraints associated with the use of multimodal models in camera trap workflows, including inference cost, API dependence, prompt sensitivity, reproducibility, and limitations for deployment in biodiversity monitoring scenarios.

\end{description}

\subsection{Intended Contributions}

The intended contributions of this thesis are as follows:

\begin{itemize}

\item An empirical evaluation of multimodal foundation models for multiple stages of the camera trap workflow, covering empty-image filtering, species classification, and behavior identification.

\item An analysis of multimodal model behavior under low-supervision scenarios, including zero-shot and few-shot settings and, for behavior identification, the use of image sequences.

\item A zero-shot multimodal fusion strategy for species classification that combines CLIP-based visual similarity with language-model-assisted textual similarity.

\item Evidence on the conditions under which multimodal models and visual-textual fusion can improve camera trap analysis, as well as the cases in which these approaches remain limited.

\item A methodological basis for reducing dependence on large annotated datasets in camera trap workflows while preserving practical relevance for biodiversity monitoring.

\end{itemize}
