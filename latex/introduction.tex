\section{Context and motivation}

Wildlife monitoring is a key information source for ecologists and biologists, underpinning a broad array of research in wildlife ecology. It aids in revealing the complex dynamics within ecosystems, assessing the effects of human activities and climate change, and informing effective species conservation measures~\citep{ahumada2013monitoring, he2016visual, tuia2022perspectives, bruce2025large, lindenmayer2022we}. Data collection for these purposes can employ a range of sensors~\citep{tuia2022perspectives}, providing multiple perspectives on animal populations. Examples include remote sensing (satellite or drone imagery, GPS tags), fixed devices (camera traps and bioacoustic recorders), and citizen-science platforms where members of the public submit observations via systems such as iNaturalist~\citep{horn2018inat, shepley2021automated, herrera2025inaturalist} and eButterfly~\citep{prudic2017ebutterfly}.

Camera traps, in particular, are an established, non-invasive, and cost-effective technique that has long been used to record wildlife in situ. The data they produce support ecological analyses of terrestrial vertebrate communities, including studies of species occupancy, diversity, abundance, density, community composition, and behavior~\citep{ahumada2013monitoring, beery2021iwildcam, tanwar2021camera, delisle2021next, burton2015wildlife}. These devices are typically triggered by motion or set to capture images at scheduled intervals, producing short image or video sequences~\citep{he2016visual}. Consequently, camera-trap deployments often generate very large datasets: for instance, the Snapshot Serengeti project collected 3.2 million images between June 2010 and May 2013 across six seasons~\citep{swanson2015snapshot}, and that collection has since grown to 7.1 million images over eleven seasons~\citep{ss_lila}. Similarly, the Caltech Camera Traps dataset contains 243,100 images from 140 camera locations in the Southwestern United States, annotated across 21 animal categories (plus empty), primarily at the species level, for example, opossum, raccoon, and coyote, with approximately 66,000 bounding box annotations. Notably, around 70\% of its images are labeled as empty~\citep{beery2018recognition}. Manual annotation of datasets of this scale is time-consuming and costly for both experts and volunteer contributors the original Snapshot Serengeti labeling required more than 17,000 volunteer hours~\citep{norouzzadeh2018automatically} and assembling comparable volunteer effort is frequently impractical for smaller studies or those focused on less charismatic species.

In the last decade, deep learning has become the dominant approach for image analysis and has been extensively applied to automate camera-trap data processing. Typical applications include filtering out empty images~\citep{mononen2025active, yousif2019animal, yang2024systematic, cunha2021filtering, norouzzadeh2018automatically, willi2019identifying, yang2021systematic, yang2021adaptive, yang2021automatic, VELASCOMONTERO2024102815, wei2020zilong, alencar2023context, alencar2024zero}, species classification~\citep{chen2023applying, velez2023evaluation, vecvanags2022ungulate, villa2017towards, beery2018recognition, norouzzadeh2018automatically, tabak2019machine, willi2019identifying, schneider2020three, kutugata2021automatic, zhu2022class, norman2023can}, individual counting~\citep{norouzzadeh2018automatically, norouzzadeh2021deep, arshad2020my, wang2022deep, simoes2023deepwild}, and behavior recognition~\citep{norouzzadeh2018automatically, schindler2021identification, bain2021automated, binta2023animal, lu2023mammalclub}. Concurrently, several software platforms have emerged to help researchers accelerate labeling and information extraction with deep-learning models~\citep{velez2023evaluation}. Some offer user-friendly interfaces that allow a wider community of camera-trap users to train custom models. For example, MegaDetector~\citep{beery2019efficient} is a general detector for animals, people, and vehicles that assists with identifying relevant frames and removing empty images, though it does not perform species-level classification. To reach biologists who favor R, the MLWIC2 package provides pre-trained models and training tools for species classification and empty-frame filtering in that language~\citep{tabak2020improving}. Other GUI-based platforms such as Wildlife Insights~\citep{ahumada2020wildlife}, Conservation AI~\citep{chalmers2019conservation}, IFLA~\citep{xi2021image}, and Animal Scanner~\citep{yousif2019animal} have likewise been developed to simplify extraction of information from camera-trap images.

These AI-driven tools have markedly increased the speed and scalability of camera-trap data processing and annotation, enabling applications at larger scales~\citep{fennell2022use, clarfeld2023evaluating, bohner2023semi, lonsinger2024efficacy, celis2024versatile, chen2024minimizing}. Studies show that species labels produced by computer-vision models can approximate expert-derived ecological metrics such as species richness, occupancy, and activity patterns~\citep{whytock2021robust, pantazis2024deep}. Nonetheless, significant obstacles persist, including acute class imbalance and challenges in generalizing across domains~\citep{beery2021scaling, yang2021systematic, zhu2022class, malik2021two, cunha2023bag}. Moreover, a large portion of camera-trap data still relies on manual processing, which limits the overall scalability of biodiversity monitoring because of the extensive human labor involved~\citep{beery2023wild}.

\section{Challenges in computer vision for camera-trap data analysis}

Extracting relevant information from camera trap images involves several difficulties that can hinder accurate analysis, even for experienced specialists. Common problems reported in the literature include animals appearing at great distances from the camera, natural camouflage, complex or unusual body postures, and inadequate lighting conditions \citep{villa2017towards, norouzzadeh2018automatically, beery2018recognition, gomez2016animal, burton2015wildlife, westworth2022understanding, mulero2025addressing, palencia2022random, mitterwallner2024automated, peral2022inappropriate}. Under such circumstances, capturing multiple images per trigger event can improve identification accuracy, as different poses may reveal distinctive morphological features necessary for species recognition \citep{beery2018recognition}.

A further complication arises from the pronounced class imbalance typically observed in camera-trap datasets. While certain species are represented by large numbers of images, others appear only rarely, resulting in a long-tailed distribution \citep{he2023long}. As noted by \citet{schneider2020three}, models tend to exhibit low recall for underrepresented species, despite the fact that these rare taxa are often of particular ecological importance.

Domain generalization also constitutes a significant challenge. Because camera traps are stationary, models can inadvertently learn correlations with site-specific backgrounds present in the training data, leading to reduced performance when deployed in new environments \citep{beery2018recognition, schneider2020three, tabak2019machine, norman2023can, liu2024reviving, rechter2025improving, amin2025automated}.

In addition, many practitioners working with camera-trap data operate under limited computational constraints, which restricts the feasibility of deploying large and resource-intensive models \citep{schneider2020three, VELASCOMONTERO2024102815, gardiner2026towards}. This underscores the need for computationally efficient methods, particularly for enabling on-device processing within the sensors themselves. Edge-based inference can offer several benefits; for example, in network-connected deployments, pre-filtering images locally can prevent the transmission of empty frames, thereby conserving bandwidth and reducing energy consumption~\citep{elias2017s}. Nevertheless, relatively few studies have explicitly examined the balance between predictive performance and inference latency on devices with restricted hardware capabilities \citep{tyden2020edge, zualkernan2020towards}. At the same time, there is increasing concern regarding the environmental costs associated with large-scale models that require substantial energy resources \citep{reynolds2024potential}.

Beyond these aspects, camera-trap datasets often contain additional complexities, including annotation errors, temporal and spatial dependencies among observations, and limited dataset sizes \citep{norouzzadeh2018automatically, schneider2020three, brookes2025panaf, bevan2025deep}. Collectively, these challenges create opportunities for the development of novel methodological approaches tailored to the specific characteristics of wildlife monitoring data.

\section{Computer vision tasks in automated analysis of camera-trap data}

Before ecologists can incorporate camera trap data into ecological models, the raw images must be processed to extract the specific information needed for the study, such as which species are present, how many individuals of each species appear in a capture event, the sex or age class of the animals (male, female, juvenile), and their observed behaviors (e.g., feeding, resting, fighting) \citep{norouzzadeh2018automatically}. The precise set of outputs needed depends on the research question or project goals, and these processing steps can be formulated as computer-vision problems that stimulate the development of tailored methods.

A widely investigated task is species recognition framed as a visual classification problem, commonly treating each image as an independent example. Although classification is a classical problem in computer vision, camera-trap datasets pose several intertwined difficulties for this task: extreme class imbalance \citep{schneider2020three, johnson2019survey, li2024deep}, few-shot learning scenarios \citep{alencar2024zero}, and fine-grained distinctions between similar species \citep{van2017devil} can all occur within the same dataset. When species-level identification is not possible, providing labels at higher taxonomic ranks still has ecological value \citep{schneider2024recognition}, which introduces an additional hierarchical dimension to the classification problem. Related objectives such as estimating age, determining sex, and recognizing activities can likewise be posed as classification tasks \citep{norouzzadeh2018automatically}.

Because most deployments capture multiple images per trigger in order to increase the odds of obtaining an informative view, another important problem is species identification using all images from a single capture event \citep{beery2018recognition, sundaresan2025adapting}. Standard workflows usually treat each photo independently during both training and inference, but exploiting the full image sequence can improve classification when discriminative markings or poses appear only in some frames. Thus, the goal becomes to infer species identity from the entire sequence associated with a capture event rather than from a lone photograph.

Given that a large fraction of camera-trap frames are empty (caused by incidental sensor activations), automating the removal of empty images is a practical task. Tools such as MegaDetector have proven effective for this purpose \citep{gadot2024crop, velez2023evaluation, leorna2022human, fennell2022use, pestell2025smart, henrich2024semi}, although filtering is frequently done post-collection. Implementing certain processing steps on the edge i.e., directly on the camera hardware could be advantageous, but requires lightweight models capable of running on constrained devices. On-device filtering reduces the volume of data sent over low-bandwidth links and lowers storage demands, thereby shortening retrieval times. While detector models (exemplified by MegaDetector) are effective at localizing animals \citep{beery2019efficient, gadot2024crop}, they tend to be computationally demanding.
