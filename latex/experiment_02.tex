\chapter{Multimodal Fusion for Species Classification in Camera Trap Workflows}\label{cap:experiment_02}

The previous chapter showed that pre-trained multimodal models can support several stages of the camera-trap workflow, but also that their performance varies substantially across tasks, model architectures, supervision regimes, input contexts, and species classes. This variability suggests that relying on a single multimodal inference path may be insufficient for robust species recognition under low-supervision conditions. Building on this observation, this chapter focuses specifically on zero-shot species classification and investigates whether combining complementary visual and textual evidence can improve recognition without task-specific training. The evaluated approach, WildMatch-VLM-Fusion, integrates CLIP-based visual similarity with text-based similarity derived from language-model-generated descriptions and species-level textual knowledge. Part of the material presented in this chapter has been accepted for publication at SIBGRAPI 2026 under the title "Zero-Shot Multimodal Fusion for Species Classification in Camera Trap Images". This chapter extends and contextualizes that work within the broader scope of the thesis

\section{Introduction}
The multi-task evaluation presented in the previous chapter showed that multimodal models are promising for camera-trap workflows, but also revealed substantial variability in their behavior. Some models performed well in specific tasks or supervision regimes, while others degraded under few-shot adaptation, sequence-based inference, or particular class conditions. In species classification, this variability was especially relevant because the models differed not only in aggregate accuracy but also in their class-wise recognition patterns. These results indicate that zero-shot species recognition may benefit from approaches that do not depend on a single model output or a single source of evidence.

Recent research has explored vision-language models as an alternative for species recognition under limited supervision. Approaches such as CLIP learn joint visual-textual representations capable of zero-shot recognition, while methods such as WildMatch perform zero-shot classification by generating visual descriptions of animals and matching them against external species knowledge. However, the findings of the previous chapter suggest that standalone multimodal inference can be unstable across models and classes. This motivates the use of fusion as a way to combine independent evidence sources instead of relying exclusively on either visual similarity or generated textual descriptions.

This chapter investigates whether zero-shot species classification can be improved by combining complementary visual and textual similarity signals. The evaluated method, WildMatch-VLM-Fusion, integrates a CLIP-based visual similarity score with a textual similarity score derived from language-model-generated descriptions matched against a species knowledge base encoded with ModernBERT. The central problem addressed is whether this training-free multimodal strategy can improve zero-shot camera trap species recognition across different datasets and image settings. The approach is compared against a WildMatch-based baseline, BLIP, and standalone CLIP to assess the specific contribution of the fusion mechanism.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Dataset and Data Characteristics}
The experiments were conducted on three camera trap datasets: Caltech Camera Traps, Snapshot Serengeti, and WCS. These datasets were selected because they represent different environments, species sets, and image acquisition conditions. For each dataset, the evaluation incorporated two image settings: full-frame images and cropped images. The cropped images correspond to regions containing the animal, which were obtained using the MegaDetector object detection model. The evaluation subsets consisted of 1000 images for Caltech, 1200 images for Serengeti, and 1000 images for WCS per image setting. No training or fine-tuning was performed on these images.

\subsection{Task Definition and Knowledge Base}
The investigated task was zero-shot species classification. For each dataset, the candidate species set was fixed based on the available labels. The selected species included:

\textbf{Caltech (10 species):} raccoon, coyote, deer, rabbit, bobcat, bird, squirrel, rodent, skunk, and lizard.

\textbf{Serengeti (12 species):} zebra, hyenaspotted, warthog, impala, elephant, giraffe, buffalo, guineafowl, ostrich, cheetah, hippopotamus, and baboon.

\textbf{WCS (10 species):} \textit{Equus quagga}, \textit{Bos taurus}, \textit{Loxodonta africana}, \textit{Mitu tuberosum}, \textit{Macaca nemestrina}, \textit{Tapirus terrestris}, \textit{Argusianus argus}, \textit{Psophia leucoptera}, \textit{Panthera onca}, and \textit{Hystrix brachyura}.

A textual knowledge base was constructed by associating each candidate species with a visually relevant summary. These summaries were derived from Wikipedia pages and manually filtered to retain only visually observable characteristics, such as body shape, fur color, and markings. Non-visual information, such as sounds, diet, and geographic distribution, was excluded to ensure the text described features visible in camera trap images.

\subsection{Models Evaluated}
The proposed WildMatch-VLM-Fusion method was compared against three reference configurations:

\textbf{Baseline:} A WildMatch-based zero-shot method that generates image descriptions and matches them against external species descriptions.

\textbf{BLIP:} A standalone BLIP configuration evaluated under the same candidate species setting.

\textbf{CLIP:} A standalone CLIP configuration that utilizes visual-text similarity without the proposed textual fusion branch.

\subsection{Inference Procedure and Fusion Strategy}
The WildMatch-VLM-Fusion pipeline consists of a visual matching branch and a textual matching branch. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} illustrates the overall architecture of the proposed approach.

\begin{figure*}[!ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=6in]{images/clip_fusion.png}
\caption{Overview of the WildMatch-VLM-Fusion pipeline. The method combines visual and textual similarity scores.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure*}

The visual branch utilizes the CLIP ViT-L/14 model. The input image is encoded into an $L_2$-normalized embedding $e_{\text{img}}$. Each species summary is prepended with a camera trap prompt and encoded by the CLIP text encoder to produce a cached embedding $e^{\text{CLIP}}_s$. The visual score $v_s$ is calculated as the cosine similarity between the image embedding and the species embedding:

$$v_s=\cos(e_{\text{img}}, e^{\text{CLIP}}_s)$$

The textual branch uses the GPT-4o-mini language model to generate natural-language descriptions of the input image. Three captions are generated per image using paraphrased prompts. Each generated description is encoded using the \texttt{answerdotai/ModernBERT-base} model. Sentence-level representations are obtained by mean-pooling the last hidden states and applying $L_2$ normalization. The species summaries are encoded using the same process. The textual score $\bar{t}_s$ for each species $s$ is computed by averaging the cosine similarities across the $n$ generated descriptions and then normalizing the result to the interval [0, 1]:

$$\bar{t}_s=\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \cos(e_{d_i}, e^{\text{BERT}}_s)$$

The final score $f_s$ is obtained through a weighted linear fusion of the normalized visual and textual scores:

$$f_s=\alpha \cdot \hat{v}_s+(1 - \alpha) \cdot \hat{t}_s$$

where $\hat{v}_s$ and $\hat{t}_s$ are the normalized visual and textual scores, and $\alpha$ is a weighting parameter. In the evaluated setup, $\alpha$ was empirically set to 0.7, assigning 70\% weight to the visual branch and 30\% weight to the textual branch. The predicted species $\hat{y}$ corresponds to the maximum fused score:

$$\hat{y}=\text{arg}\max_s f_s$$

No model weights are updated during this process, maintaining a strict zero-shot regime.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics and Statistical Reporting}
Model performance was evaluated primarily using top-1 classification accuracy across all dataset-image configurations. To assess statistical significance, the Friedman test was applied to the aggregate accuracies across the six configurations, treating each configuration as a block. The Nemenyi post hoc test was subsequently used to identify pairwise differences between the evaluated methods.

The aggregate top-1 accuracy results show that WildMatch-VLM-Fusion achieved the highest accuracy in four of the six evaluated settings: Caltech cropped, Serengeti cropped, WCS cropped, and WCS full-frame. The only configuration where the proposed method performed below the Baseline was Caltech full-frame images, with a small decrease of -0.10 percentage points.

Compared to the Baseline, WildMatch-VLM-Fusion achieved an average improvement of 2.18 percentage points across all configurations. The largest gains were observed in Caltech cropped images (+5.10 percentage points) and WCS full-frame images (+4.30 percentage points).

Standalone CLIP demonstrated competitive performance, particularly on Caltech cropped images where it achieved 72.70\% accuracy. However, WildMatch-VLM-Fusion outperformed standalone CLIP across all evaluated settings, with the largest margin observed on WCS full-frame images (76.40\% vs. 69.50\%). Standalone BLIP consistently produced the lowest accuracies, ranging from 2.90\% to 12.90\% across the different configurations.

In total, the fusion approach improved accuracy in 37 of the 64 species-image configurations, matched the baseline in two configurations, and decreased performance in 25 configurations.

For cropped images, positive changes were observed for several classes. In the Caltech dataset, accuracy for rodent increased from 34.0\% to 67.0\%, and deer improved from 91.0\% to 95.0\%. However, the accuracy for raccoon decreased from 86.0\% to 54.0\%. Similar variations occurred in the WCS dataset, where \textit{Mitu tuberosum} improved from 30.0\% to 64.0\%, while \textit{Psophia leucoptera} decreased substantially from 77.0\% to 14.0\%.

For full-frame images, WildMatch-VLM-Fusion improved the rodent class in Caltech from 21.0\% to 77.0\%, but decreased raccoon from 96.0\% to 55.0\%. In the Serengeti dataset, cheetah improved from 77.0\% to 95.0\%, while zebra decreased from 80.0\% to 58.0\%.

The analysis suggests that the proposed method acts primarily as a selective refinement mechanism. It frequently improved classes that already exhibited moderate to high baseline accuracy, such as Caltech cropped deer and Serengeti cropped ostrich. It also produced substantial gains for some difficult classes, such as \textit{Mitu tuberosum} in WCS full-frame images. Concurrently, the method caused performance drops for certain high-performing baseline classes when the fused visual and textual evidence became misleading.

\section{Discussion}
The results demonstrate that integrating visual and textual similarity scores can improve aggregate zero-shot species classification performance across most of the evaluated dataset and image configurations. The consistent outperformance of WildMatch-VLM-Fusion over standalone CLIP indicates that the textual branch contributes relevant semantic information not captured by direct visual-text similarity.

However, the statistical analysis mandates a conservative interpretation. The observed accuracy gains are empirical improvements within the evaluated settings, but do not represent a statistically significant superiority over the Baseline or standalone CLIP models based on the selected tests.

The effect of image cropping varied depending on the dataset. For the Caltech and Serengeti datasets, cropped images yielded higher fusion accuracy than full-frame images, which aligns with the expectation that removing background clutter increases the salience of the animal. Conversely, full-frame images produced higher accuracy for the WCS dataset, indicating that broader contextual information or specific crop qualities can influence model performance in certain environments.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. First, the evaluation was strictly zero-shot, without train/test splits or domain adaptation, which restricts conclusions about how the method might behave if fine-tuning were applied. Second, the textual branch relied on mean-pooled ModernBERT embeddings, rather than an embedding model specifically optimized for sentence-level semantic similarity. Third, the Friedman and Nemenyi tests were computed over only six aggregate dataset-image configurations; this small number of blocks limits statistical power and likely contributes to the absence of significant differences between the Baseline, CLIP, and WildMatch-VLM-Fusion, independently of the true effect size. Finally, the approach depends heavily on the quality of both the externally sourced species summaries and the language-model-generated image descriptions.

\section{Final Remarks}
This chapter investigated WildMatch-VLM-Fusion, a zero-shot multimodal fusion method for species classification in camera trap workflows. The method explicitly combines CLIP-based visual similarity with text-based similarity matching without relying on target-task training data.

The experimental results show that the fusion approach achieved the highest accuracy in four out of six configurations and produced an average improvement of 2.18 percentage points over the reference baseline. However, species-level analyses confirmed that the benefits of fusion are highly class-dependent, acting as a selective refinement mechanism that can improve certain challenging classifications while simultaneously degrading others.

Within the context of this thesis, this experiment demonstrates that complementary multimodal signals can be combined to reduce reliance on annotated training data, but it also highlights that the effectiveness of such training-free integration is subject to environmental variables, species characteristics, and the underlying quality of the generated textual knowledge.
