\chapter{Multimodal Fusion for Species Classification in Camera Trap Workflows}\label{cap:experiment_02}

\section{Introduction}

Camera trap monitoring is an important tool for biodiversity assessment, but the large volume of images produced by these systems makes manual annotation costly and time-consuming. Automated species classification can support ecological workflows by reducing the effort required to inspect and label collected images. This chapter addresses species classification in camera trap images under a zero-shot setting, that is, without task-specific training or fine-tuning on the target datasets.

The method under study, referred to as \textit{WildMatch-VLM-Fusion}, integrates complementary visual and textual signals in a multimodal inference pipeline. The first signal is obtained directly from the image by means of a vision-language model (VLM), which in this work refers to CLIP, and which measures similarity between the input image and textual species descriptions represented in a shared vision-language embedding space. The second signal is derived from natural-language descriptions of the image generated by a large language model (LLM), which in this work refers to GPT-4, and which are subsequently compared against species descriptions encoded with ModernBERT. These two score vectors are then combined through a weighted linear fusion strategy to produce the final species ranking.

A central characteristic of the proposed approach is that all components operate with pre-trained weights and no gradient-based optimisation is performed for the target task. The pipeline therefore relies on the combination of a pre-built textual knowledge base, image-caption generation, visual-text similarity, and score fusion to support classification. The relevance of this experiment lies in evaluating whether such a training-free multimodal strategy can improve zero-shot camera trap species recognition across different datasets and image settings, including both full-frame and cropped images. For comparison, the proposed method is evaluated against a baseline zero-shot multimodal approach based on the WildMatch framework \citep{fabian2023multimodal}, which relies on vision-language caption generation and matching against external species descriptions without explicit multimodal score fusion.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Pipeline Overview, Inputs, and Data Resources}

The evaluated system is a zero-shot multimodal pipeline designed for animal species classification in camera trap images. For each input image, the method combines: (i) a visual similarity signal obtained from a VLM, and (ii) a textual similarity signal derived from an LLM-generated image description encoded with ModernBERT and matched against species profiles stored in a knowledge base. The final prediction is produced by a weighted linear combination of the two modalities. Because neither the VLM nor the text encoder is adapted to the target domain, the method does not require labelled training data for the evaluated classification task (see Figure \ref{fig:clip_fusion}).

For reference, the baseline method used in the experiments follows the WildMatch framework \citep{fabian2023multimodal}, which performs zero-shot classification by generating detailed captions of camera trap images using a vision-language model and matching these descriptions against species profiles derived from external knowledge sources such as Wikipedia. The approach incorporates instruction-tuned caption generation, hierarchical classification, and self-consistency across multiple captions, but does not explicitly combine independent visual and textual similarity signals through a fusion mechanism.

The pipeline takes two inputs for each prediction: a camera trap image and a pre-built knowledge base. Two image variants are considered in the evaluation: full-frame images and cropped images, where the latter correspond to regions containing the animal as obtained through an animal detection step. The knowledge base is represented as a structured JSON resource mapping each candidate species name to a set of textual fields. The key element used during inference is the \textit{Visually Relevant Summary} (VRS), defined as a paragraph-length description of the species' visible appearance. These summaries are generated offline by prompting an LLM (GPT-4o-mini) to extract only photographically observable information from the corresponding Wikipedia article, while excluding non-visual attributes (e.g., measurements, sounds, and smells) and returning a single paragraph.

The experiments are conducted on three camera-trap datasets (Caltech, Serengeti, and WCS). For each dataset, classification is performed over a fixed candidate set corresponding to the species available in that dataset subset. The same candidate set is used to define the class labels and to construct the species entries in the knowledge base used during inference.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Serengeti (12 species, common names):} zebra, hyenaspotted, warthog, impala, elephant, giraffe, buffalo, guineafowl, ostrich, cheetah, hippopotamus, baboon.
\item \textbf{WCS (10 species, scientific names):} \textit{Equus quagga}, \textit{Bos taurus}, \textit{Loxodonta africana}, \textit{Mitu tuberosum}, \textit{Macaca nemestrina}, \textit{Tapirus terrestris}, \textit{Argusianus argus}, \textit{Psophia leucoptera}, \textit{Panthera onca}, \textit{Hystrix brachyura}.
\item \textbf{Caltech (10 species, common names):} raccoon, coyote, deer, rabbit, bobcat, bird, squirrel, rodent, skunk, lizard.
\end{itemize}

Image preprocessing is minimal and delegated to the VLM preprocessing transform. The input image is resized, centre-cropped, and normalised according to the model's expected input distribution. No additional augmentation, denoising, or domain-specific preprocessing is applied. The image is loaded and converted to RGB before being passed to the visual encoder.

\subsection{Multimodal Inference Method}

The pipeline consists of three main components: a vision-language model for caption generation, a VLM-based visual matching branch, and a ModernBERT-based textual matching branch.

A vision-capable large language model (LLM), specifically GPT-4o-mini, is used to generate natural-language descriptions of the input image. Rather than relying on a single fixed instruction, the system samples one prompt from a set of paraphrased variants, all aimed at eliciting a detailed visual description of the animal in the photo. This prompt randomisation is intended to encourage descriptive diversity when multiple captions are requested. Each caption is generated with a maximum budget of 500 tokens. In the evaluated configuration, three captions are generated for each image.

The visual branch uses a pre-trained VLM (CLIP ViT-L/14). The VLM projects both images and texts into a shared 768-dimensional embedding space, allowing similarity computation between the input image and textual species descriptions. Let $\mathbf{e}_{\text{img}}$ denote the $\ell_2$-normalised embedding of the input image. Each species summary from the knowledge base is prepended with a generic camera-trap prompt and encoded through the VLM text encoder. These text embeddings are computed once and cached for subsequent reuse. For each candidate species $s$, the visual similarity score is defined as the cosine similarity between the image embedding and the cached VLM text embedding for that species:
\begin{equation}
v_s = \cos\bigl(\mathbf{e}_{\text{img}},\; \mathbf{e}^{\text{VLM}}_s\bigr).
\end{equation}
This score quantifies the alignment between the visual content of the image and the species-specific textual description represented in the VLM embedding space.

The textual branch uses \texttt{answerdotai/ModernBERT-base} model. To obtain fixed-length sentence representations, the last hidden state is mean-pooled across all tokens and then $\ell_2$-normalised. Each caption generated by the vision-language model is encoded into a ModernBERT embedding. Similarly, each VRS in the knowledge base is encoded and cached. For each caption $d_i$, cosine similarity is computed against all species embeddings. If $n$ captions are generated, the per-species textual score is obtained by averaging the cosine similarities across captions:
\begin{equation}
\bar{t}_s = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\cos\bigl(\mathbf{e}_{d_i},\; \mathbf{e}^{\text{BERT}}_s\bigr).
\end{equation}
After averaging, the textual score vector is min--max normalised to the interval $[0,1]$.

The final species ranking is obtained by combining the visual and textual score vectors through a weighted linear fusion. When score normalisation is enabled, both the VLM visual scores and the ModernBERT textual scores are independently min--max normalised to $[0,1]$. The fused score for species $s$ is then computed as:
\begin{equation}
f_s = \alpha \cdot \hat{v}_s + (1-\alpha)\cdot \hat{t}_s,
\end{equation}
where $\hat{v}_s$ and $\hat{t}_s$ are the normalised visual and textual scores, respectively, and $\alpha \in [0,1]$ controls the contribution of each modality. In the evaluated configuration, $\alpha = 0.7$, assigning 70\% of the final score to the VLM visual branch and 30\% to the textual branch. The predicted label is the species associated with the maximum fused score:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y} = \arg\max_s f_s.
\end{equation}

In contrast, the baseline method does not perform such weighted fusion; instead, it relies on matching generated captions directly to species descriptions, optionally incorporating multiple captions and hierarchical reasoning strategies as proposed in \citep{fabian2023multimodal}.

In addition to the predicted label, the method outputs a confidence value based on the relative margin between the two highest fused scores. Let $f_{(1)}$ and $f_{(2)}$ denote the highest and second-highest fused scores, respectively. The confidence is defined as:
\begin{equation}
c = \frac{f_{(1)}}{f_{(1)} + f_{(2)}}.
\end{equation}
This formulation yields values in the interval $(0.5, 1]$. Scores close to 1 indicate a large separation between the top-ranked and second-ranked candidates, whereas a value near 0.5 corresponds to a near tie.

For each test image, the inference process follows five steps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The vision-language model generates $n$ captions for the input image.
\item The image is encoded with the VLM, and cosine similarities are computed against the cached VLM text embeddings of all candidate species.
\item Each generated caption is encoded with ModernBERT, cosine similarities are computed against the cached textual embeddings of all species, and the resulting scores are averaged across captions and normalised.
\item The visual and textual score vectors are independently normalised and fused through the weighted linear combination defined above.
\item The species with the highest fused score is returned as the final prediction together with the confidence estimate.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Experimental Protocol and Configuration}

No training, fine-tuning, or parameter optimisation is performed in the evaluated system. The VLM (CLIP ViT-L/14), ModernBERT-base, and the LLM (GPT-4o-mini) are used with their original pre-trained parameters. The fusion weight $\alpha$ is fixed rather than learned. The construction of the knowledge base is executed as an offline preprocessing step and does not involve gradient-based learning. Accordingly, the complete method operates in a zero-shot regime with respect to the target species classification task.

Evaluation is conducted on the full set of available images for each dataset configuration. No train/test split is performed. The primary metric is top-1 classification accuracy, computed as the proportion of images for which the predicted species matches the ground-truth label. For each image, the system records the true label, predicted label, confidence score, and modality-specific scores for subsequent analysis. The reported experimental results compare the proposed VLM Fusion approach against the WildMatch-based baseline \citep{fabian2023multimodal} across three datasets---Caltech, Serengeti, and WCS---and under two image settings, namely cropped and full-frame images.

The evaluated configuration uses the following settings:
\begin{itemize}
\item VLM model: CLIP ViT-L/14;
\item text encoder: ModernBERT-base;
\item LLM for caption generation: GPT-4o-mini;
\item number of captions per image: $n=3$;
\item fusion weight: $\alpha = 0.7$;
\item score normalisation: min--max scaling to $[0,1]$;
\item VLM text prompt: a generic camera-trap prefix applied to species summaries;
\item caption generation temperature: 0.7 when $n>1$;
\item maximum caption length: 500 tokens.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experiments and Results}

\subsection{Results Across Datasets and Image Settings}

The experiments evaluate the behaviour of the proposed multimodal pipeline across six principal configurations formed by the combination of three datasets (Caltech, Serengeti, and WCS) and two image types (cropped and full-frame images). For each configuration, results are reported for four approaches: the WildMatch-based baseline, BLIP, standalone CLIP, and the proposed CLIP Fusion pipeline. The primary metric reported is top-1 classification accuracy, together with the total number of evaluated samples and the corresponding number of correct predictions.

Table~\ref{tab:exp02_accuracy_all_methods} summarises the complete set of top-1 accuracy results. The proposed CLIP Fusion method obtained the highest accuracy in four of the six configurations: Caltech cropped, Serengeti cropped, WCS cropped, and WCS full. In Serengeti full-frame images, CLIP Fusion matched the baseline performance exactly, with both methods achieving 66.42\% accuracy. The only configuration in which CLIP Fusion was slightly below the baseline was Caltech full-frame images, where the difference was marginal, corresponding to $-0.10$ percentage points.

The standalone CLIP results provide an additional reference for interpreting the contribution of the fusion mechanism. In Caltech cropped images, CLIP alone already achieved a strong result, with 72.70\% accuracy, but CLIP Fusion further improved this value to 73.40\%. In the remaining configurations, CLIP Fusion consistently outperformed standalone CLIP. This suggests that the incorporation of the textual branch can provide additional discriminative information beyond visual-text matching with CLIP alone, particularly in the WCS full-frame setting, where CLIP Fusion improved over standalone CLIP by 6.90 percentage points.

By contrast, BLIP showed substantially lower performance across all configurations, with accuracies ranging from 2.90\% to 12.90\%. These results indicate that, under the evaluated zero-shot classification protocol, BLIP alone was not competitive with the baseline, standalone CLIP, or the proposed fusion approach. This behaviour suggests that BLIP, when used in isolation under this evaluation setup, was not sufficient for the fine-grained species classification task considered in this chapter.

\section{Discussion}

The complete set of results provides a more detailed view of the contribution of multimodal fusion to zero-shot species classification in camera trap images. The proposed CLIP Fusion method achieved the best accuracy in four of the six evaluated dataset--image configurations and tied with the baseline in one additional configuration. The only case in which the baseline remained slightly superior was Caltech full-frame images, where the difference was limited to 0.10 percentage points. Overall, these results indicate that the fusion of CLIP-based visual similarity and ModernBERT-based textual similarity can improve zero-shot recognition performance in most evaluated settings.

The comparison with standalone CLIP is particularly informative. Although CLIP alone achieved competitive performance in some configurations, especially Caltech cropped images, CLIP Fusion outperformed it in every dataset--image configuration. This suggests that the textual branch contributes useful complementary information when combined with the visual branch through weighted fusion. The improvement is especially notable for WCS full-frame images, where CLIP Fusion reached 76.40\% accuracy compared with 69.50\% for standalone CLIP and 72.10\% for the baseline. This result indicates that the proposed fusion mechanism may be particularly beneficial when contextual visual information from the full frame remains relevant to classification.

The effect of cropping was not uniform across datasets. For Caltech and Serengeti, cropped images produced higher CLIP Fusion accuracy than full-frame images. This is consistent with the expectation that removing background clutter can make the target animal more visually salient. However, the opposite pattern was observed for WCS, where full-frame images achieved substantially higher accuracy than cropped images for both the baseline and CLIP Fusion. This suggests that, in WCS, contextual information or crop quality may play an important role. The results therefore indicate that cropping is not universally beneficial and that the relationship between image framing and classification accuracy depends on dataset-specific characteristics.

The BLIP results show that this approach performed poorly under the evaluated protocol. Across all datasets and image types, BLIP accuracy remained below 13\%, with the lowest value observed for WCS full-frame images. This outcome suggests that BLIP, when used without an effective species-level matching or fusion strategy, is insufficient for the fine-grained zero-shot classification task considered in this chapter.

Overall, the results support the feasibility of a training-free multimodal classification pipeline for camera trap species recognition. The proposed CLIP Fusion method improves over the baseline in most configurations and consistently outperforms standalone CLIP and BLIP. However, the gains are dataset- and image-type-dependent. The findings therefore support the use of multimodal fusion as a promising strategy for zero-shot camera trap classification, while also highlighting the importance of evaluating performance across multiple datasets and image representations.

Some limitations remain. First, the pipeline performs no train/test split and evaluates on the available images for each configuration. Consequently, the reported results are limited to this evaluation setup and do not assess generalisation under separate training and testing partitions. Second, the ModernBERT representations are obtained by mean-pooling token embeddings rather than by using a sentence-embedding model specifically optimised for semantic similarity. Third, the method depends on an offline-generated knowledge base whose visually relevant summaries are derived from Wikipedia through LLM prompting. The effect of summary quality on final classification performance is not isolated in the present experiment. Finally, although multiple captions are generated, the implemented strategy averages textual similarity scores before fusion rather than performing label-level majority voting, which should be considered when interpreting the role of caption diversity.

\section{Final Remarks}

This chapter described a zero-shot multimodal experiment for species classification in camera trap images based on the \textit{WildMatch-VLM-Fusion} pipeline. The method combines a CLIP-derived visual similarity signal with a textual similarity signal obtained from LLM-generated image descriptions and ModernBERT-based semantic matching against species summaries stored in a knowledge base. Final predictions are produced through weighted linear fusion, with no training or fine-tuning performed for the target task.

The complete evaluation showed that CLIP Fusion achieved the highest accuracy in four out of six dataset--image configurations and tied with the baseline in one additional configuration. The average accuracy improvement over the baseline was approximately 2.18 percentage points. The strongest improvements were observed for Caltech cropped images and WCS full-frame images. In contrast, the only slight decrease was observed for Caltech full-frame images, where CLIP Fusion was 0.10 percentage points below the baseline.

The inclusion of standalone BLIP and CLIP results provides additional context for interpreting the proposed approach. BLIP performed poorly across all configurations, whereas standalone CLIP achieved competitive results but was consistently outperformed by CLIP Fusion. These findings indicate that the fusion mechanism provides benefits beyond the use of CLIP alone and that the combination of visual and textual similarity signals is more effective than either BLIP or CLIP as isolated components under the evaluated protocol.

Within the context of this thesis, the experiment contributes evidence that multimodal pre-trained models can be integrated into camera trap workflows without requiring task-specific model training. The results indicate that such integration can improve zero-shot species classification in several settings, while also highlighting limitations related to dataset dependence, image framing, encoder choice, and the construction of species-level textual knowledge bases.
