Biodiversity monitoring using camera traps has become an essential tool for ecological studies. However, the manual analysis of the vast volume of data generated by these devices remains a significant challenge. In recent years, deep learning-based methods have been widely used to automate tasks such as filtering empty images, species recognition, and identifying animal behavior. Despite these advances, such approaches often rely on large volumes of annotated data and face generalization limitations in real-world scenarios. This proposal focuses on investigating the conditions under which multimodal machine learning models—particularly vision-language models—can support the automation of camera trap workflows in low-supervision settings. To this end, the work evaluates the potential of these models in core biodiversity monitoring tasks and examines strategies for combining visual and textual information without relying on task-specific training. The first stage investigates the capability of multimodal models to perform three fundamental tasks: filtering empty images, species classification, and behavior identification. These tasks are analyzed in zero-shot and few-shot settings, also considering inference based on image sequences. Results indicate that multimodal models can achieve competitive performance without task-specific training, although their effectiveness varies depending on the task, model architecture, and the use of temporal context. In a second stage, this work proposes and examines a multimodal fusion strategy for species classification in a zero-shot setting. The approach combines CLIP-based visual similarity with textual similarity derived from descriptions generated by language models. Results show that integrating visual and textual signals can improve classification accuracy across different scenarios, although gains depend on data characteristics and the application context. Overall, this proposal contributes empirical and methodological evidence regarding the use of pre-trained multimodal models in camera trap workflows. In particular, the work demonstrates the potential of these models to reduce reliance on annotated data, evaluates their limitations across various biodiversity monitoring tasks, and proposes a visual-textual fusion strategy to support species classification in zero-shot scenarios.
