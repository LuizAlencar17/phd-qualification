Biodiversity monitoring using camera traps has become an essential tool for ecological studies. However, the manual analysis of the vast volume of data generated by these devices remains a significant challenge. In recent years, deep learning-based methods have been widely used to automate tasks such as filtering empty images, species recognition, and identifying animal behavior. Despite these advances, such approaches often rely on large volumes of annotated data and face generalization limitations in real-world scenarios. This proposal focuses on investigating the conditions under which multimodal machine learning models—particularly vision-language models—can support the automation of camera trap workflows in low-supervision settings. To this end, the work evaluates the potential of these models in core biodiversity monitoring tasks and examines strategies for combining visual and textual information without relying on task-specific training. The first stage investigates the capability of multimodal models to perform three fundamental tasks: filtering empty images, species classification, and behavior identification. These tasks are analyzed in zero-shot and few-shot settings, also considering inference based on image sequences. Results indicate that multimodal models can achieve competitive performance without task-specific training, although their effectiveness varies depending on the task, model architecture, and the use of temporal context. In a second stage, this work proposes and examines a multimodal fusion strategy for species classification in a zero-shot setting. The approach combines CLIP-based visual similarity with textual similarity derived from descriptions generated by language models. Results show that integrating visual and textual signals can improve classification accuracy in most evaluated scenarios, although the gains are dataset- and species-dependent and do not reach statistical significance against the strongest baselines. Building on the instability observed across standalone multimodal models in the first stage, and on the partial, task-specific gains obtained through fixed-weight fusion in the second stage, this proposal specifies WildBind as its third and most integrative contribution. WildBind is a unified multimodal binding architecture that reformulates species classification and behavior identification as a single hierarchical retrieval problem over a shared ecological ontology, while relying on a pretrained object detector to resolve empty-image filtering upstream. Instead of a fixed combination weight, it fuses an arbitrary number of visual, textual, and contextual evidence sources through a learned, uncertainty-aware fusion gate. Consistent with the proposal stage of this research, the architecture, training procedure, and evaluation protocol of WildBind are fully specified, although empirical results are not yet available. Overall, this proposal contributes empirical and methodological evidence on the use of pre-trained multimodal models in camera trap workflows, together with a novel unified architecture that generalizes these findings into a single learned mechanism. Specifically, the work (i) demonstrates the potential of pre-trained multimodal models to reduce reliance on annotated data, (ii) evaluates their limitations across distinct biodiversity monitoring tasks, (iii) proposes a visual-textual fusion strategy for zero-shot species classification, and (iv) specifies WildBind as a low-supervision alternative that unifies species classification and behavior identification through the learned, uncertainty-aware fusion of heterogeneous evidence sources, building on a pretrained detector that resolves empty-image filtering, jointly covering the three investigated tasks.
