Biodiversity monitoring using camera traps has become an essential tool for ecological studies; however, the manual analysis of the large volume of data generated by these devices still represents a significant challenge. In recent years, deep learning-based methods have been widely used to automate tasks such as empty-image filtering, species recognition, and animal behavior identification. Despite these advances, such approaches generally depend on large volumes of annotated data and still present limitations in generalizing to real-world scenarios. This thesis centrally investigates under which conditions multimodal artificial intelligence models, especially vision-language models, can support the automation of camera-trap workflows under low-supervision regimes. To this end, the work evaluates the potential of these models in core biodiversity monitoring tasks and examines strategies for combining visual and textual information without relying on task-specific training. The first empirical evaluation investigates the ability of multimodal models, including CLIP, BLIP, GPT, and Gemini, to perform three fundamental tasks: empty-image filtering, species classification, and behavior identification. These tasks are analyzed under zero-shot and few-shot settings, also considering sequence-based image inference. The results indicate that multimodal models can achieve competitive performance without task-specific training, although their effectiveness varies according to the task, model architecture, availability of examples, and use of temporal context. The second evaluation proposes and examines a multimodal fusion strategy for zero-shot species classification. The approach combines CLIP-based visual similarity with textual similarity derived from descriptions generated by language models. The results show that integrating visual and textual signals can improve top-1 accuracy across different scenarios, although the gains depend on data characteristics and the application context. Overall, this thesis contributes empirical and methodological evidence on the use of pre-trained multimodal models in camera-trap workflows. In particular, the work demonstrates the potential of these models to reduce dependence on annotated data, evaluates their limitations across distinct biodiversity monitoring tasks, and proposes a visual-textual fusion strategy to support species classification in zero-shot scenarios.
