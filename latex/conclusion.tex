\chapter{Project Proceedings}\label{cap:conclusion}

This chapter summarizes the scientific progress achieved so far and defines the remaining research required to complete the doctoral thesis. The thesis investigates the conditions under which pre-trained multimodal models can support the automation of camera trap workflows for biodiversity monitoring under low-supervision and zero-shot settings. The research has progressed through two completed empirical studies and a third, more integrative contribution that remains to be empirically developed and evaluated.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:project_conclusion}

The research conducted so far establishes a progression from the evaluation of standalone multimodal models toward the development of a unified multimodal binding approach for camera trap understanding.

The first empirical study evaluated CLIP, BLIP, GPT-4o, and Gemini across three core camera trap tasks: empty-image filtering, species classification, and behavior identification. The results demonstrated that pre-trained multimodal models can perform these tasks without task-specific training in several configurations, but their effectiveness is highly conditional on the model, task, supervision regime, input context, and species or behavior class. Few-shot adaptation substantially improved BLIP, while GPT and Gemini achieved competitive zero-shot performance in specific tasks. Sequence-based inference also improved behavior identification for some models, but its effect was not uniform. These findings therefore provide evidence for the potential of multimodal foundation models while simultaneously demonstrating that no single model or inference configuration is consistently effective across the complete workflow.

The second empirical study investigated whether complementary visual and textual information could improve zero-shot species classification. WildMatch-VLM-Fusion combined CLIP-based visual similarity with textual similarity derived from language-model-generated image descriptions and species-level textual knowledge. The method achieved the highest accuracy in four of six evaluated dataset and image configurations and obtained an average improvement of 2.18 percentage points over the reference baseline. However, the statistical analysis did not establish significant superiority over the Baseline or standalone CLIP. Moreover, the gains were class-dependent and the fusion mechanism relied on a manually selected fixed weight, $\alpha=0.7$, between exactly two evidence sources. Thus, the second study provides evidence that multimodal fusion can be useful, but also exposes limitations in the current formulation of fusion.

Together, these studies establish the empirical foundation for the next contribution of the thesis. The first study shows that independent multimodal inference is heterogeneous across tasks and models, while the second shows that complementary evidence can partially reduce this variability but remains restricted to a single task and a fixed fusion rule. Consequently, the central remaining research problem is not simply to further optimize either of the previous experiments, but to develop a mechanism capable of learning how heterogeneous ecological evidence should be combined across the camera trap workflow.

This motivates the third contribution of the thesis: \textit{WildBind}, a unified multimodal binding architecture for joint zero-shot camera trap understanding. WildBind reformulates empty-image filtering, species classification, and behavior identification as hierarchical retrieval problems over a shared \textit{Ecological Ontology Graph}. Instead of using a manually fixed combination of two signals, the proposed architecture learns to integrate an arbitrary number of visual, textual, temporal, and contextual evidence sources through an uncertainty-aware fusion gate. The architecture is designed to preserve the low-supervision character of the thesis while providing a common mechanism for the three investigated tasks.

The scientific contribution of the thesis is therefore not limited to demonstrating that existing multimodal models can be applied to camera trap data. Rather, the completed research is intended to progress from empirical characterization of these models, through evidence for multimodal complementarity, toward a unified learned mechanism for binding heterogeneous ecological evidence. WildBind constitutes the main remaining methodological contribution required to complete this progression.

\section{Contributions}\label{sec:contrib}

The contributions of the thesis are organized into two completed empirical contributions and one methodological contribution that remains to be empirically validated.

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Multimodal evaluation of camera trap workflows.} An empirical evaluation of pre-trained multimodal models across empty-image filtering, species classification, and behavior identification, covering zero-shot and few-shot settings and, for behavior identification, single-image and sequence-based inference.

\item \textbf{Characterization of multimodal model variability.} An analysis of how model architecture, supervision regime, temporal context, image characteristics, and ecological classes affect multimodal performance in camera trap workflows.

\item \textbf{Zero-shot visual-textual fusion for species classification.} A training-free multimodal fusion strategy that combines CLIP-based visual similarity with textual similarity derived from language-model-generated image descriptions and species-level knowledge.

\item \textbf{Empirical evidence on multimodal complementarity.} An evaluation showing that complementary visual and textual evidence can improve zero-shot species classification in several camera trap scenarios, while also demonstrating that the gains are class-dependent and not uniformly statistically significant.

\item \textbf{WildBind: unified multimodal binding for camera trap understanding.} A unified architecture that represents empty-image filtering, species classification, and behavior identification as hierarchical retrieval over a shared Ecological Ontology Graph and integrates multiple visual, textual, temporal, and contextual evidence sources through a learned, uncertainty-aware fusion mechanism.

\item \textbf{Empirical validation of unified multimodal binding.} The remaining experimental contribution will determine whether the proposed learned binding mechanism can improve the robustness and generality of zero-shot camera trap understanding compared with standalone multimodal models and fixed-fusion approaches.

\end{itemize}

\section{Current Status of the Project}\label{sec:current_status}

The thesis has completed the two empirical studies that establish its initial scientific contributions. The first study characterized the capabilities and limitations of pre-trained multimodal models across multiple camera trap tasks, while the second investigated the complementarity of visual and textual evidence for zero-shot species classification. These studies provide the empirical basis for the formulation of the third contribution.

The main contribution that remains to be completed is the empirical development and validation of WildBind. The architecture, training procedure, Ecological Ontology Graph, fusion mechanism, and evaluation protocol have already been specified in the proposed third study. However, empirical results are not yet available, and therefore the claims concerning the effectiveness of WildBind remain hypotheses to be tested rather than demonstrated results.

The remaining research is consequently centered on determining whether the proposed unified binding mechanism can translate the findings of the first two studies into a general method for joint zero-shot camera trap understanding.

\section{Next Steps}\label{sec:next_steps}

The remaining research activities are organized around the development and empirical validation of WildBind, followed by the final consolidation of the doctoral research.

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Development and implementation of WildBind.}

The proposed architecture will be implemented according to the specification presented in Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_03}. This stage will establish the complete experimental pipeline, including the Ecological Ontology Graph, the visual and textual projection components, the multimodal binding objective, the uncertainty-aware fusion gate, and the mechanisms required to operate when individual evidence sources are unavailable. The implementation will preserve the low-supervision setting by keeping the underlying pre-trained multimodal backbones frozen and concentrating task-specific learning on the proposed binding components.

\item \textbf{Training and experimental validation.}

WildBind will be trained and evaluated to determine whether the proposed binding mechanism can jointly support empty-image filtering, species classification, and behavior identification. The experiments will assess the contribution of multimodal binding under different combinations of visual, textual, temporal, and contextual evidence and will compare the proposed approach against the reference methods established in the previous chapters.

\item \textbf{Ablation, robustness, and comparative analysis.}

The experimental analysis will investigate which components of WildBind are responsible for its performance. In particular, the study will examine the contribution of the shared ecological representation, the multimodal binding objective, the learned fusion gate, and the different evidence sources. The analysis will also assess robustness to missing modalities and variation in image context, species classes, and task conditions. These experiments are necessary to determine whether the proposed architecture provides advantages beyond the fixed two-branch fusion strategy investigated in Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_02}.

\item \textbf{Scientific consolidation and dissemination.}

The results of the WildBind experiments will be consolidated into the final methodological contribution of the thesis and prepared as a journal manuscript. The final thesis will then integrate the three contributions into a single research narrative, followed by thesis writing and defense.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Final Remarks}\label{sec:final_remarks_project}

The thesis is not yet complete in terms of scientific contribution. The first two studies have established the empirical basis for the research by characterizing the capabilities and limitations of pre-trained multimodal models and by demonstrating that complementary visual and textual evidence can provide gains in zero-shot species classification. However, these studies also reveal limitations that motivate a more general solution: standalone models behave inconsistently across tasks, while the existing fusion strategy is restricted to one task, two evidence sources, and a manually selected fixed combination weight.

The remaining stage of the research therefore focuses on WildBind as the principal methodological contribution of the thesis. The proposed architecture generalizes the multimodal fusion principle to the complete set of camera trap tasks investigated in this work and replaces fixed fusion with a learned, uncertainty-aware mechanism capable of integrating heterogeneous evidence sources. The architecture has been specified, but its effectiveness remains to be established empirically.

The completion of this contribution is essential for answering the broader research question of the thesis: not only whether pre-trained multimodal models can be used in camera trap workflows, but whether their complementary information can be systematically bound into a unified, low-supervision mechanism capable of supporting multiple ecological vision tasks. The final thesis will therefore be completed only after the proposed method has been implemented, experimentally evaluated, critically analyzed, and incorporated into the overall scientific synthesis.
