\chapter{Unified Multimodal Binding for Joint Camera Trap Understanding}\label{cap:experiment_03}

The previous two chapters established two complementary but partial findings. Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_01} showed that pre-trained multimodal models can support empty-image filtering, species classification, and behavior identification, but that no single model, supervision regime, or input context is uniformly superior across the three tasks: each task was evaluated with its own prompts, its own candidate label set, and its own scoring pipeline. Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_02} showed that explicitly combining two complementary sources of information---CLIP-based visual similarity and language-model-generated textual similarity---can improve species classification over either source alone, but the fusion mechanism was a single global weight ($\alpha=0.7$) tuned by hand, applied to exactly two evidence sources, and restricted to one task.

This chapter proposes and specifies WildBind, a unified multimodal binding architecture intended to complement these two findings. Rather than treating species classification and behavior identification as two independently engineered pipelines, WildBind represents both as retrieval problems over a single shared Ecological Ontology Graph (EOG) embedded in a common latent space, applied to camera-trap events already flagged as containing an animal; empty-image filtering itself is resolved directly by a detector's confidence threshold, rather than by a further retrieval step. Rather than combining two evidence sources with a fixed linear weight, WildBind learns a compact, uncertainty-aware gating function that adaptively weighs an arbitrary number of visual, textual, temporal, and sequence-level evidence sources, and that degrades gracefully when any of them is unavailable. Unlike WildMatch-VLM-Fusion, WildBind is not a zero-shot method: it relies on a label-free contrastive pretraining stage and on a small labeled set used to train the gate, while all large pretrained encoders remain frozen. The remainder of this chapter specifies the architecture and training procedure, and details the planned experimental evaluation, including datasets, baselines, metrics and statistical tests.

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Scope: Event-Level, Post-Acquisition Processing}\label{sec:wildbind_scope}

Before specifying the WildBind architecture, we explicitly state two scope assumptions that constrain its practical application.

First, WildBind operates on camera-trap events -- the short bursts of consecutively triggered images sharing the same motion or interval trigger, as introduced in Section~\ref{sec:camera-traps-workflow} -- rather than on single images considered independently of their acquisition context. Every modality integrated by the architecture is defined at the level of the event rather than the frame. These modalities are: (1) the visual anchor, a single crop selected from the event; (2) the generated-description embedding $e_{cap}$, produced from that same crop; (3) the burst-context embedding $e_{seq}$, which pools information across the event; and (4) the spatiotemporal embedding $e_{ctx}$, which describes properties of the event as a whole. The visual anchor is not equivalent to single-frame inference, since selecting the anchor itself requires scanning the full event, and $e_{cap}$ inherits this event-level selection because it is generated from the anchor crop. Consequently, WildBind produces exactly one species prediction and one behavior prediction per event, and images that cannot be grouped into events fall outside its intended scope.

Second, WildBind is proposed as an offline, workflow-level processing stage rather than an on-device or embedded model. It is intended to run after images have already been retrieved from the camera-trap devices and organized into events (Section~\ref{sec:camera-traps-workflow}), and before ecological indicators are derived -- occupying the same position in the workflow as the tasks investigated in Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_01} -- rather than the acquisition stage targeted by edge-deployed classifiers such as the one proposed by Zualkernan et al.~\citep{zualkernan2022iot}. This assumption follows directly from the computational profile of the architecture: although the base encoders --- the visual encoder, the text encoder, and the pretrained detector --- remain frozen and only lightweight projection heads and the fusion gate are trained (Section~\ref{sec:contrastive_binding}), running three separate pre-trained backbones per event, together with VLM-based caption generation for $e_{cap}$, exceeds the memory and power budget of the embedded platforms considered in edge-oriented camera-trap pipelines. WildBind is therefore best understood as a candidate replacement for the manual screening and annotation stage of the workflow described in Section~\ref{sec:camera-traps-workflow}, executed in batch on a server or workstation, and not as a substitute for the in-camera triggering mechanism itself.

\subsection{Motivation}

The multi-task evaluation in Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_01} and the fusion study in Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_02} converge on the same diagnosis: standalone multimodal inference is unstable across models, tasks, and classes, and even an explicit two-branch fusion strategy only partially stabilizes this behavior. At the same time, the literature reviewed in Chapter~\ref{cap:related_work} shows a second, related gap: general-purpose unified vision-language systems for camera traps, exemplified by CameraTrap-Instruct \citep{chen2026cameratrap}, achieve high multi-task performance (species identification, counting, and behavior classification from a single instruction-tuned model) but do so by fine-tuning a large vision-language backbone (Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct with LoRA adapters) on an attribute-rich, manually curated instruction dataset. This reintroduces exactly the dependency on large annotated, task-specific data that motivates the low-supervision perspective of this thesis (Hypotheses H1--H3, Chapter~\ref{cap:introduction}).

Consequently, this chapter addresses the following question: How can multiple heterogeneous evidence sources be integrated into a single learned representation to jointly cover species classification and behavior identification -- for events already flagged as containing an animal by a pretrained detector -- while keeping supervision low and remaining robust to missing modalities at inference time?

\subsection{Inspiration from Multimodal Binding Outside Ecology}

A structurally similar problem has recently been addressed outside biodiversity monitoring, in the prediction of protein function from heterogeneous biological modalities. Boadu et al. \citep{boadu2026funbind} propose FunBind, a multimodal model that aligns protein sequence, structure, textual description, and domain-annotation embeddings in a single contrastively learned latent space, using the sequence modality as a central anchor because it is the only modality guaranteed to be available for every protein. After self-supervised contrastive pretraining, FunBind supports two complementary prediction regimes from the same backbone: an unsupervised zero-shot regime, in which a candidate functional term is scored purely by embedding similarity to the anchor modality, and a supervised regime, in which lightweight classification heads are fine-tuned on top of the frozen, aligned representations and their per-modality predictions are fused by weighted averaging. Since every non-anchor modality is optional, the model tolerates missing modalities at both training and inference time, and the fusion weights are optimized on held-out data rather than fixed by hand.

WildBind adapts this design principle to the camera-trap domain. The role of the protein sequence as an always-available anchor is played by the visual embedding of the event's most informative frame. The role of structure, text, and domain annotations is played by burst-level visual context, generated image descriptions, and spatiotemporal metadata; and the role of Gene Ontology terms as the prediction target is played by an Ecological Ontology Graph that spans the species-classification and behavior-identification tasks investigated in this thesis. Of FunBind's two regimes, WildBind is closer to the supervised one, since its fusion step is trained on labeled data; it differs in that no classification heads are trained: candidates are scored by similarity in the frozen shared space, and only the fusion weights are learned. While the domains differ fundamentally and ecological semantics are far less standardized than Gene Ontology, the underlying architectural principles transfer directly. Specifically, anchor-centered contrastive binding, support for optional modalities, and a learned fusion step precisely address the three structural gaps identified above.

\subsection{Design Overview}

WildBind is organized around a central visual anchor and a set of optional peripheral modalities, all mapped into a shared latent space through modality-specific frozen encoders followed by small trainable projection heads. Table~\ref{tab:wildbind_modalities} summarizes the modalities and their encoders. Because every peripheral modality is contrastively bound to the visual anchor (Section~\ref{sec:contrastive_binding}), the criterion used to select it directly shapes the rest of the architecture. For a given event, a pretrained detector is applied independently to every frame in the burst, following the same crop-generation step already used to build the cropped-image setting of Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_02}. Among all detections obtained across the event, the frame containing the single highest-confidence animal-class bounding box is selected as the anchor frame. The box is converted to a square region whose side is its largest dimension multiplied by a margin factor $\rho$ before cropping, a margin intended to retain contextual pixels around the animal and avoid truncating body parts at the box boundary. The initial configuration uses $\rho=1.2$ (a 10\% margin on each side), a moderate margin chosen to keep surrounding context without diluting the animal in the crop. The final value will be determined experimentally on validation data, together with a sensitivity analysis (Section~\ref{sec:hyperparameters}). The resulting crop is encoded by the visual encoder to obtain $e_v$.

Detector confidence plays a dual role in WildBind: it selects the visual anchor, and it directly determines the event's presence/absence label. If the highest-confidence detection across the event reaches or exceeds an acceptance threshold $\theta_{det}$, the event is classified as containing an animal, and that detection is converted into the anchor crop as described above. If no detection in the event reaches $\theta_{det}$, the event is classified as empty at the detector level and the WildBind retrieval pipeline described in Section~\ref{sec:fusion_gate} is not invoked: no species or behavior prediction is produced for that event. The threshold controls a trade-off: lowering it reduces the number of animal events that are discarded irrecoverably, but increases the number of empty events that reach the retrieval stage and receive a spurious species and behavior label, since the EOG contains no node for empty scenes. The initial configuration uses $\theta_{det}=0.5$, the midpoint of the confidence range, as a neutral starting point. The final value will be determined experimentally on validation data by examining this trade-off, and the sensitivity of the complete pipeline to it will be reported (Section~\ref{sec:hyperparameters}).

This design choice is intentional: rather than treating presence/absence as a further retrieval decision over the Ecological Ontology Graph (EOG, Section~\ref{sec:eog}), WildBind delegates it entirely to the pretrained detector already required to localize the anchor, avoiding a duplicated decision and keeping the EOG scoped to disambiguating species and behavior once an animal has already been localized. The direct consequence of this choice -- namely, that a missed detection can no longer be recovered from peripheral evidence, unlike in a design in which presence is resolved by fusing all available modalities -- is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations_discussion}.

\paragraph{Training and inference stages.} All base encoders (visual encoder, text encoder, and detector) remain frozen throughout. The learned components of WildBind are trained in two successive, offline stages, and inference then requires no parameter updates:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Contrastive binding pretraining} (Section~\ref{sec:contrastive_binding}). The projection heads and the learned components of the peripheral modalities (the attention pooling of $e_{seq}$ and the MLP of $e_{ctx}$) are trained to align each peripheral modality with the visual anchor of the same event. This stage uses no species or behavior labels.
\item \textbf{Gate training} (Section~\ref{sec:gate_training}). With the encoders and projection heads frozen, the fusion gate $g_\theta$ is trained once on a small labeled calibration set.
\item \textbf{Inference} (Section~\ref{sec:inference}). Predictions are obtained through forward passes only; no parameter of any component is updated.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contrastive Binding and Self-Supervised Pretraining}
\label{sec:contrastive_binding}

The central mechanism used to align the heterogeneous evidence sources in WildBind is contrastive binding. Rather than requiring the different modalities to share the same encoder, each modality is independently projected into a common latent space. The visual event embedding serves as the anchor representation, while the corresponding sequence, generated description, and spatiotemporal context embeddings provide complementary representations of the same camera-trap event, as explained in this section. In this stage the base encoders are frozen and only the projection heads and the learned components of the peripheral modalities are optimized, using unlabeled camera-trap events. These events are drawn from the training camera locations of Snapshot Serengeti (seasons 1--6, following the location-level separation of Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_01}) and from camera locations of Caltech Camera Traps and WCS that do not contribute images to the evaluation subsets of Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_02}. The held-out dataset described in Section~\ref{sec:datasets} is never used in this stage.

For a given event $i$, the visual embedding $e_v^i$ and the corresponding
modality embedding $e_m^i$ form a positive pair. In contrast, modality
embeddings associated with other events in the same minibatch form negative
pairs (Figure ~\ref{fig:contrastive_binding}). The contrastive objective therefore encourages representations
associated with the same event to become close in the shared latent space,
while separating representations originating from different events.

\begin{figure*}[!ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=6.5in]{images/experiment_03/contrastive_binding_infonce.png}
\caption{Contrastive multimodal binding with InfoNCE. Matching visual and modality embeddings from the same camera-trap event are pulled together, while embeddings from different events are pushed apart. The contrastive objective is applied across modalities to learn a shared latent representation.}
\label{fig:contrastive_binding}
\end{figure*}

For each visual--modality pair, the similarity between the visual anchor and
a modality embedding is computed using temperature-scaled cosine similarity:

\begin{equation}
S_{ij}^{(m)}
=
\frac{
\cos\left(e_v^i,e_m^j\right)
}{\tau},
\label{eq:similarity_temperature}
\end{equation}

where $e_v^i$ denotes the visual anchor embedding of event $i$,
$e_m^j$ denotes the embedding of modality $m$ associated with event $j$,
and $\tau$ is a learnable temperature parameter controlling the sharpness
of the similarity distribution.

For a minibatch containing $N$ events, the InfoNCE objective for modality
$m$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{v,m}
=
-\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\log
\frac{
\exp\left(S_{ii}^{(m)}\right)
}{
\sum_{j=1}^{N}
\exp\left(S_{ij}^{(m)}\right)
}.
\label{eq:infonce}
\end{equation}

The numerator corresponds to the positive pair associated with the same
camera-trap event, whereas the denominator contains the positive pair and
all mismatched modality embeddings in the minibatch. Minimizing
$\mathcal{L}_{v,m}$ therefore increases the relative similarity between
matched visual--modality representations while reducing the similarity
between representations originating from different events.

The binding objective is applied independently to each peripheral modality
and subsequently aggregated into a single multimodal objective:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{bind}
=
\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}
\lambda_m\mathcal{L}_{v,m},
\label{eq:binding_loss}
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{M}=\{\mathrm{seq},\mathrm{cap},\mathrm{ctx}\}$ denotes the set of peripheral modalities and $\lambda_m\ge 0$ are fixed coefficients that control the contribution of each modality to the overall binding objective. They are hyperparameters rather than learned parameters, since minimizing $\mathcal{L}_{bind}$ with respect to unconstrained $\lambda_m$ would trivially drive them to zero. They are initialized uniformly ($\lambda_m=1$ for all modalities), and their final values will be determined experimentally on validation data (Section~\ref{sec:hyperparameters}). This objective encourages the visual anchor to remain consistently aligned with the heterogeneous evidence sources while preserving a shared latent representation that can subsequently be used by the uncertainty-aware fusion mechanism.

The text projection head learned in this stage, which maps the text-encoder output of the generated captions into the shared space, is the same head later used to embed the descriptions of the EOG nodes (Section~\ref{sec:eog}). This is what places the EOG nodes in the same latent space as the event modalities.

\subsection{Retrieval, Fusion Gate, and Inference}\label{sec:fusion_gate}

Before formalizing the fusion mechanism, it is useful to state what is learned and when. No component of WildBind is trained to output a species or behavior label directly. Instead, every prediction is obtained through a two-step operation applied to a set of candidate EOG nodes: (i) a retrieval step, in which each available modality embedding is compared, via cosine similarity, against the embeddings of the candidate nodes; and (ii) a fusion step, in which a trained gate $g_\theta$ combines the resulting per-modality similarity scores into a single fused score per candidate node. The predicted label is the candidate with the highest fused score, and the same operation is applied independently to the species nodes and to the behavior nodes.

Two sets of parameters are therefore learned, at different moments. The projection heads are learned during contrastive pretraining without labels (Section~\ref{sec:contrastive_binding}) and are frozen afterwards. The gate $g_\theta$ is learned in a separate, offline stage on a small labeled calibration set (Section~\ref{sec:gate_training}) and is also frozen at inference time. Inference itself never updates any parameter (Section~\ref{sec:inference}). Labeled data are thus exposed only to the parameters of $g_\theta$. Extending the label set with a previously unseen species or behavior requires adding its textual description to the corresponding knowledge base and training the gate on the extended label set (Section~\ref{sec:limitations_discussion}); the base encoders and projection heads are not retrained.

\subsubsection{The Ecological Ontology Graph}\label{sec:eog}

Species classification and behavior identification are reformulated as retrieval over a single Ecological Ontology Graph (EOG), which contains two sets of nodes embedded in the same latent space (Figure ~\ref{fig:wildbind_eog}):

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Species nodes} ($\mathcal{S}$). One node per candidate species, described by the visually oriented summaries of the Species Knowledge Base.
\item \textbf{Behavior nodes} ($\mathcal{B}$). One node per behavior class (moving, eating, resting, extendable to additional classes), described by the textual descriptions of the Behavior Knowledge Base.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure*}[!ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=6in]{images/experiment_03/eog.png}
\caption{The Ecological Ontology Graph (EOG). It comprises two independent sets of nodes, species nodes $\mathcal{S}$ and behavior nodes $\mathcal{B}$, each defined by a short textual description from the corresponding knowledge base. Descriptions are embedded by the frozen text encoder followed by the text projection head learned during contrastive pretraining, which places all nodes in the same latent space as the event modalities. No dependency is imposed between the two sets.}
\label{fig:wildbind_eog}
\end{figure*}

No dependency is imposed between the two predictions: the candidate set for behavior identification is always the full set $\mathcal{B}$, regardless of the predicted species.

\paragraph{Knowledge bases.} Each node is defined by a short natural-language description. A knowledge base (KB) is the collection of such descriptions for one node set; it is a fixed textual input to the model, not a learned component. The Species Knowledge Base (SKB) is the one built in Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_02}: for each candidate species, a summary extracted from its Wikipedia page and manually filtered to retain only visually observable characteristics (e.g., body shape, fur color, and markings), excluding non-visual information such as vocalizations, diet, and geographic distribution. The Behavior Knowledge Base (BKB) contains one description for each behavior class. The descriptions are written manually following the same criterion adopted for the SKB: they retain only visually observable cues of the behavior (e.g., body posture, head position, and limb configuration) and exclude non-visual information. Since the wording of node descriptions is itself a design choice, its influence on the results will be examined in the experiments.

\paragraph{Node embeddings.} Every node description is embedded by the frozen text encoder and then by the text projection head learned during contrastive pretraining (Section~\ref{sec:contrastive_binding}), yielding a node embedding $e_c$ in the shared latent space. No node-specific parameters are trained: descriptions are processed in exactly the same way as the generated captions. This is the mechanism that unifies species classification and behavior identification: instead of separate classifiers, WildBind performs the same operation---similarity scoring against a set of candidate node embeddings---for both node sets.

\subsubsection{Retrieval and Gate-Based Fusion}

Let $\mathcal{C}$ denote the set of candidate nodes under consideration ($\mathcal{S}$ or $\mathcal{B}$), and let $\mathcal{M}^{+}=\mathcal{M}\cup\{v\}$ be the set of all modalities, including the visual anchor. Each available modality $m\in\mathcal{M}^{+}$ produces its own similarity score against every candidate node $c\in\mathcal{C}$:

$$s_c^{(m)} = \cos\!\left(e_m,\, e_c\right).$$

This generalizes the two-branch score $f_s=\alpha \hat v_s+(1-\alpha)\hat t_s$ of Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_02} (Equation therein) to an arbitrary number of modalities. Rather than a fixed $\alpha$, the modality weights are produced by a lightweight gating network $g_\theta$:

$$z = g_\theta\big([\,u\,;\,a\,]\big), \qquad w_m = \frac{a_m \exp(z_m)}{\sum_{m'\in\mathcal{M}^{+}} a_{m'} \exp(z_{m'})}, \qquad f_c = \sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}^{+}} w_m \, \hat{s}_c^{(m)},$$

where $u$ is a vector of per-modality uncertainty statistics (probability margin and normalized entropy of the modality's own ranking over $\mathcal{C}$, computed directly from the similarity scores $s_c^{(m)}$); $a \in \{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{M}^{+}|}$ is a binary modality-availability mask, with $a_v=1$ because the visual anchor is always available; and $\hat{s}_c^{(m)}$ denotes the min--max normalized score of modality $m$ across the candidates in $\mathcal{C}$. Since the unavailable modalities ($a_m=0$) are removed from both the normalization and the weighted sum, the weights of the available modalities always sum to one, so predictions can degrade gracefully when a modality is missing. A single gate $g_\theta$ is shared by the species and behavior node sets. Because the uncertainty statistics $u$ are computed over each candidate set, the gate yields a separate weight vector $w$ for each node set and each event, while its inputs (margins, entropies, and the availability mask) do not depend on the identity of the classes. The predictions are

$$\hat{y}_{\mathrm{sp}} = \arg\max_{c \in \mathcal{S}} f_c, \qquad \hat{y}_{\mathrm{beh}} = \arg\max_{c \in \mathcal{B}} f_c,$$

computed independently from the same event evidence.

\subsubsection{Gate Training}\label{sec:gate_training}

The gate $g_\theta$ is the only component trained with labeled data. Its training is performed once, offline, after contrastive pretraining and before inference, with the base encoders and projection heads frozen. A single gate is trained on the pooled calibration data of all in-distribution datasets, so that it can be applied unchanged to the held-out dataset (Section~\ref{sec:datasets}). The calibration set is small, labeled, and disjoint from all evaluation data. It is drawn from the validation camera locations of Snapshot Serengeti (species and behavior labels, following the splits of Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_01}) and from camera locations of Caltech Camera Traps and WCS that do not contribute images to the evaluation subsets of Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_02} (species labels only). Its size will be determined experimentally by varying the number of labeled events (Section~\ref{sec:hyperparameters}).

For each labeled calibration event, the similarity scores $s_c^{(m)}$ are computed for every available modality and candidate, the uncertainty statistics $u$ and the availability mask $a$ are derived from them, and the fused scores $f_c$ are obtained as described above. The parameters $\theta$ are optimized by minimizing the cross-entropy between the ground-truth node and the softmax of the fused scores over the candidates, summed over the node sets for which a label is available:

$$\mathcal{L}_{gate} = -\sum_{\mathcal{C}\in\{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{B}\}} \log \frac{\exp\left(f_{c^{*}}/\tau_g\right)}{\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}} \exp\left(f_{c}/\tau_g\right)},$$

where $c^{*}$ is the ground-truth node of the event in $\mathcal{C}$ and $\tau_g$ is a fixed temperature, treated as a hyperparameter, that rescales the fused scores. To expose the gate to missing modalities, the peripheral modalities are randomly dropped during training: for each calibration event, each peripheral modality is independently marked unavailable with probability $p_{drop}$, while the visual anchor is always kept. Since only $\theta$ is trained, the training cost and labeled-data requirements are much smaller than those of fine-tuning a vision-language model.

\subsubsection{Inference}\label{sec:inference}

At inference time all parameters (base encoders, projection heads, and gate) are frozen, and each event is processed as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Presence check.} The event is classified as containing an animal by the pretrained detector during anchor selection (Section~\ref{sec:wildbind_scope}): the highest-confidence detection across the event reached the acceptance threshold $\theta_{det}$. Events that did not reach this threshold are discarded as empty before reaching WildBind, so the steps below are executed only for animal-flagged events.

\item \textbf{Modality extraction.} The visual anchor is selected and encoded into $e_v$. The peripheral embeddings $e_{seq}$, $e_{cap}$, and $e_{ctx}$ are computed for every modality available for this event; any modality that cannot be computed for a given event (e.g., a missing timestamp) is marked unavailable in the modality-availability mask $a$ rather than imputed with a default value.

\item \textbf{Species retrieval and fusion.} Every available modality embedding is scored against the species nodes $\mathcal{S}$. The gate combines these per-modality scores into a fused score $f_c$ for each node, and $\hat{y}_{\mathrm{sp}}=\arg\max_{c\in\mathcal{S}} f_c$ is taken as the species prediction.

\item \textbf{Behavior retrieval and fusion.} The same procedure is applied, independently of step 3, to the behavior nodes $\mathcal{B}$, yielding the behavior prediction $\hat{y}_{\mathrm{beh}}$.
\end{enumerate}

No parameter of the base visual or textual encoders is updated at any stage, and no parameter of any component is updated at inference time.

\subsection{Implementation Plan}\label{sec:implementation_plan}

To keep the architectural specification independent of any particular pretrained checkpoint, Sections~\ref{sec:wildbind_scope}--\ref{sec:fusion_gate} refer to the frozen encoders only by their functional role (visual encoder, text encoder, pretrained detector); the choice of checkpoint for each role is treated as an implementation configuration rather than as part of the proposed method. For concreteness and reproducibility, the planned initial components are: CLIP ViT-L/14~\citep{radford2021learning} for the visual encoder, used for both the visual-anchor ($e_v$) and burst-context ($e_{seq}$) embeddings; ModernBERT-base~\citep{warner2024modernbert} for the text encoder, used for both the generated-description embedding ($e_{cap}$) and the EOG node embeddings (Section~\ref{sec:eog}); and MegaDetector v5~\citep{beery2019efficient} for the pretrained detector used for the presence/absence decision and for anchor-frame and crop selection. Any of the three roles can be substituted with a different frozen backbone of matching modality without changing the architecture described above, since only the projection heads and the fusion gate are retrained. The captions for $e_{cap}$ are generated from the anchor crop by GPT-4o-mini, following the captioning procedure of Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_02}, so that the textual evidence is directly comparable with that of WildMatch-VLM-Fusion; this captioning model is not trained and can likewise be replaced.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Selection}\label{sec:hyperparameters}

The specification above contains several hyperparameters whose values cannot be justified a priori: the crop margin $\rho$, the detector threshold $\theta_{det}$, the binding coefficients $\lambda_m$, the gate temperature $\tau_g$, the modality-dropout probability $p_{drop}$, and the size of the labeled calibration set. The values mentioned in the previous sections are initial configurations; the final values will be determined experimentally. All selections will be made on validation data and never on evaluation data, and the sensitivity of the results to each hyperparameter will be reported as part of the ablation analysis (Section~\ref{sec:next_steps}). For the detector-level choices ($\rho$ and $\theta_{det}$), the experiments will measure their end-to-end effect on the species and behavior predictions.

\section{Planned Experimental Evaluation}

\subsection{Datasets}\label{sec:datasets}

The evaluation reuses the datasets already curated for this thesis and adds one held-out dataset never used during pretraining or gate calibration, to test cross-domain generalization rather than in-distribution gains:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Snapshot Serengeti} (seasons 1--6, Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_01} splits) for empty-image filtering (performed by the detector within the pipeline), species classification, and behavior identification, and the Serengeti subset used in Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_02} for species classification with full-frame and cropped settings.
\item \textbf{Caltech Camera Traps and WCS} (Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_02} subsets), reused for species classification and, subject to available annotation, for empty-image filtering.
\item \textbf{One fully held-out dataset} (candidates: ENA24-detection or the Wellington Camera Traps dataset), used exclusively at test time and excluded from contrastive pretraining and from gate calibration, to measure the accuracy gap between in-distribution and genuinely unseen deployments.
\end{itemize}

For the first two datasets, the events used for contrastive pretraining and gate calibration come from camera locations that do not contribute events to the evaluation: for Snapshot Serengeti, following the location-level splits of Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_01}; for Caltech Camera Traps and WCS, from camera locations that do not contribute images to the evaluation subsets of Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_02}. A single gate is trained on the pooled calibration data and applied unchanged to the held-out dataset.

\section{Discussion: Positioning and Limitations}\label{sec:limitations_discussion}

WildBind is deliberately scoped as a methodological and architectural contribution rather than a claim of universal superiority. Three limitations should be acknowledged from the outset of the proposal.

First, WildBind is not training-free. Besides the label-free contrastive pretraining, the gate requires a small labeled calibration set, which reintroduces a modest amount of task-specific supervision that the training-free methods of the previous chapters avoided. We consider this a justified trade-off: the gate has far fewer parameters than a fine-tuned vision-language model, and all heavy multimodal base encoders remain frozen. The planned evaluation will quantify how much labeled data the gate actually needs by varying the size of the calibration set.

Second, the Ecological Ontology Graph assumes a closed candidate set for each node set. Adding a new species or behavior therefore requires adding a textual description to the corresponding knowledge base and training the gate on the extended label set. The need for a textual description is shared with WildMatch-VLM-Fusion and with the embedding-similarity prediction of FunBind, whereas the need to train the gate is specific to WildBind.

Third, because presence/absence is decided solely by the pretrained detector's confidence threshold $\theta_{det}$, WildBind directly inherits any systematic detection failures of that model, with no downstream recovery mechanism. If the detector fails to produce a confident detection in any frame of an event that in fact contains an animal -- for example, a small, camouflaged, or heavily occluded individual, a known failure mode discussed in Section~\ref{sec:camera-traps-workflow} -- the event is classified as empty and is never passed to the species/behavior retrieval stage, however informative the peripheral modalities (burst context, generated description, spatiotemporal metadata) might have been for that particular event. This is a stronger dependency than a design in which presence/absence is itself resolved by fusing all available evidence: here, a missed detection is an unrecoverable false negative for the whole pipeline, rather than an error that peripheral evidence has a chance to correct. This dependency is carried over from the same detection pipeline already used to build the cropped-image setting in Chapter~\ref{cap:experiment_02}, and is accepted here as an explicit design trade-off: it removes presence/absence from the set of decisions the fusion gate must learn, at the cost of making the overall pipeline's recall on rare or hard-to-detect animals entirely dependent on the frozen detector's own recall.

\section{Final Remarks}

This chapter proposed WildBind, a unified multimodal binding architecture for camera-trap understanding that treats species classification and behavior identification as retrieval problems over a shared Ecological Ontology Graph -- applied to events already flagged as containing an animal by a frozen, pretrained detector -- and that fuses an arbitrary number of visual, sequence, textual, and contextual evidence sources through a learned, uncertainty-aware gate rather than a fixed weight. WildBind is not zero-shot: it requires label-free contrastive pretraining and a small labeled calibration set for the gate, but all heavy encoders remain frozen and labeled supervision is confined to the gate. Within the context of this thesis, this chapter is intended to constitute the third and most conceptually integrative contribution. In Chapter~\ref{cap:conclusion}, we conclude this qualification proposal by presenting the project proceedings, consolidating the findings of the completed empirical studies, and defining the remaining research plan, ablation experiments, and timeline to complete and defend the doctoral thesis.
